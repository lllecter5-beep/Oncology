📌 01 · What Is Hereditary Cancer? ⭐⭐⭐
Core Concept
  • Person inherits a damaged gene from a parent
  • Does NOT mean born with cancer
  • Means born with a significantly higher lifetime risk
  • Germline mutation is present in every cell from birth
  • Clinical testing available for over 50 syndromes
🧠 High risk ≠ certainty. Not all carriers develop cancer.
Three Cancer Pattern Types
  • Sporadic (~75%) — random; aging + environment; no family pattern
  • Familial (~15–20%) — clusters in families; no clear single gene; genetic testing usually uninformative
  • Hereditary (5–10%) — identifiable germline mutation; vertical pedigree; early onset; multiple relatives
Key: Familial clustering ≠ Hereditary syndrome. Always distinguish between them!

🧬 02 · DNA, RNA & Molecular Biology ⭐⭐
Central Dogma: "DNA makes RNA and RNA makes protein" — guides production of protein for ALL body functions.
DNA Structure
  • 46 chromosomes = 23 pairs, one from each parent
  • Short arm = "p" arm | Long arm = "q" arm
  • DNA = double helix of two nucleotide chains held by hydrogen bonds
  • Purines: Adenine (A) + Guanine (G)
  • Pyridines: Thymine (T) + Cytosine (C)
  • Complementary pairs: A–T and G–C
  • RNA = single chain; Uracil (U) replaces Thymine
Transcription & Translation
  • Transcription = DNA → RNA
  • → Loosening histone = allows transcription
  • → Tightening histone = prevents transcription
  • Translation = RNA → Protein (amino acids)
  • A codon = 3 mRNA nucleotides (e.g., ACG = threonine)
  • Change in 1st codon position → usually different amino acid
  • Change in 3rd codon position → rarely causes amino acid change
  • Multiple codons can code for the same amino acid
🧠 Transcription = DNA→RNA | Translation = RNA→Protein
Genes
  • Individual units of hereditary information at specific chromosome positions
  • Exons = protein-coding segments of a gene
  • Introns = non-protein-coding segments (interrupting pieces)
  • SNP — Single Nucleotide Polymorphism: change in one nucleotide; occurs at variable frequency in the population; NOT always disease-causing
Types of Regulatory Genes
  • Proto-oncogenes: normal genes for cell growth (e.g., HER2). Mutations → oncogene activation → uncontrolled division
  • Tumor suppressor genes: regulate cell growth (e.g., p53, RB1). Mutation → uncontrolled growth
  • DNA repair genes: keep DNA free of errors. MMR genes = Lynch syndrome
  • Mutator gene phenotype: poor proofreading → thousands of mutations vs normal cells

🔄 03 · Epigenetics & Gene Regulation ⭐
What Is Epigenetics?
  • Switching genes on/off with "chemical tails" attached to DNA without changing the DNA sequence itself
  • Opening histone structure = allows transcription (gene ON)
  • Closing histone structure = prevents transcription (gene OFF)
  • Result: alters "DNA → RNA → protein" outcome
🧠 Epigenetics = expression change WITHOUT DNA sequence change
Factors & Clinical Relevance
  • Factors that affect epigenetics: diet, exercise, aging, drugs, addiction, parenting, environment
  • Epigenetic changes can cause OR prevent cancer
  • Explains why identical twins with the same DNA can have different cancer outcomes
  • Target for cancer treatment (e.g., histone deacetylase inhibitors)

⚡ 04 · Mutation Types ⭐⭐⭐
Germline Mutation
  • In egg or sperm (germ cell)
  • Present in every cell of the body
  • Can be passed to children (50% if AD)
  • Causes hereditary cancer syndromes
🧠 "Born with it"
Somatic Mutation
  • In body cells — occurs during life
  • Only in the cancer cell and descendants
  • Cannot be passed to children
  • Cause: tobacco, radiation, aging, random errors
  • Responsible for ~90–95% of all cancers
🧠 "Developed later"

🧩 05 · Inheritance Patterns ⭐⭐⭐
Autosomal Dominant (AD) AD
  • Only ONE abnormal copy needed to express disease
  • Heterozygous: one mutant + one normal allele
  • 50% chance of passing to each child
  • Appears in every generation — vertical pedigree pattern
  • Men and women equally affected
  • No skipping of generations (incomplete penetrance can mimic this)
🧠 ALL major hereditary cancer syndromes are AD except MUTYH (AR)
Autosomal Recessive (AR) AR
  • TWO abnormal copies required (one from each parent)
  • Parents are usually unaffected carriers
  • 25% chance of affected child when both parents are carriers
  • Horizontal pedigree — siblings affected, parents often not
  • Family history may be less obvious
  • Very rare in hereditary cancer
🧠 Only AR colorectal cancer syndrome: MUTYH (MAP)

Family History Data to Collect
  • Degree of relatives:
  • → FDR (1st degree): parents, siblings, children
  • → SDR (2nd degree): grandparents, aunts/uncles
  • → 3rd degree: cousins, great-aunts/uncles
  • Cancer history: type, bilateral/unilateral, age at diagnosis, treatment
  • Reproductive history (female): menarche, menopause, OCP use, pregnancies

  • Screening: mammogram, colonoscopy, PSA — age and frequency
  • Race/ethnicity: ALL grandparents — AJ ancestry is key risk flag
  • Pathology reports should confirm patient-reported cancer diagnoses

🤝 08 · Genetic Counseling & The Oncology Nurse ⭐⭐⭐
Definition: A communication process dealing with human problems associated with the occurrence or risk of a genetic disorder in a family.
General Oncology Nurse
  • Construct and evaluate pedigrees
  • Educate patients and families about genetic risks and cancer prevention
  • Integrate new genetic info into practice
  • Continue personal education in genetics
  • Collaborate with specialists for comprehensive, culturally sensitive care
Advanced Practice Nurse (Genetics)
  • Comprehensive cancer genetic risk assessment
  • Education, facilitation, and interpretation of genetic testing
  • Pre- and post-test counseling + follow-up
  • Tailored cancer risk management options
  • Psychosocial counseling and support services
Nursing Credentials
  • APNG — Advanced Practice Nurse in Genetics (Master's degree)
  • GCN — Genetics Clinical Nurse (Baccalaureate)
  • Administered by ANCC since 2014
  • Organizations: ONS, ISONG, ANA
🧠 ANCC = American Nurses Credentialing Center
Pre-test vs. Post-test Counseling
Pre-test Topics
  • Natural history of the disorder
  • Benefits, risks, and limitations of testing
  • How heredity contributes; recurrence risk in relatives
  • Alternatives for dealing with the risk
  • Financial implications and discrimination concerns (GINA)
  • Implications of sharing results with relatives
  • Optimal psychological adjustment for family
  • Informed Consent obtained before any testing
Post-test Topics
  • Full explanation of result: positive, negative, VUS, or uninformative
  • Individualized surveillance and screening schedule
  • Risk-reduction options: prophylactic surgery, chemoprevention
  • Implications for biological relatives
  • Psychosocial support and referrals
  • Plans for ongoing follow-up care
  • Update counseling as new evidence emerges

🧪 09 · Interpretation of Genetic Test Results ⭐⭐⭐
✅ True Positive
  • A known deleterious mutation IS found
  • Initiates individualized surveillance and screening program
  • Risk-reduction options discussed in detail
  • Biological relatives offered targeted testing for that specific mutation
  • Relatives who test negative = True Negative → relieved of intensive screening
🧠 Best practice: Test the AFFECTED family member FIRST!
⚠️ Uninformative Negative ⭐ EXAM
  • No mutation detected — does NOT rule out a hereditary syndrome
  • Occurs when first person tested does NOT have a cancer diagnosis
  • Possible reasons:
  • → Technology limitation: mutation present but undetectable
  • → Unknown gene: causative gene not yet discovered
  • → Phenocopy: tested person's cancer was sporadic
⚠️ Negative ≠ Safe. Always interpret in full clinical context!
VUS — Variant of Unknown Significance: A sequence variation whose association with disease risk is unknown. Do NOT make surveillance or surgical decisions based on VUS alone. VUS classifications are regularly updated — patients need re-contact as reclassification occurs.

🎗️ 11 · BRCA / HBOC Syndrome ⭐⭐⭐
🎗️

Hereditary Breast & Ovarian Cancer (HBOC)

Genes: BRCA1 (17q21) + BRCA2 (13q12.3) · Autosomal Dominant · Carrier: ~1/400 · Both are Tumor Suppressor Genes

Core Biology
  • 10% of breast cancers + 14% of ovarian cancers linked to a susceptibility gene
  • BRCA1: chr 17q21 — 1,064 known pathogenic mutations
  • BRCA2: chr 13q12.3 — 1,120 known pathogenic mutations
  • Normal function: respond to DNA damage → slow cell cycle → recruit repair proteins
  • Mutated: DNA damage accumulates → cancer
🌟 Ashkenazi Jewish (AJ) Founder Mutations
  • ① BRCA1 185delAG
  • ② BRCA1 5382insC
  • ③ BRCA2 6174delT
  • → Account for 80–90% of all BRCA mutations in AJ hereditary families
  • AJ ancestry = HIGH-RISK FLAG for referral
  • Other founder populations: Dutch, French Canadian, Icelandic (BRCA2 999del5)
Cancer Risk Comparison

Ovarian (to age 70)39–46%11–17%BRCA1 significantly higher

Referral Clues (Affected Individual)
Red Flags
  • Breast cancer before age 50
  • Triple-negative breast cancer at any age
  • Ovarian cancer at any age
  • Male breast cancer
  • Two or more primary breast cancers in one individual
  • Breast AND ovarian cancer in the same person
  • Pancreatic or prostate cancer (Gleason >7)
  • AJ ancestry
Family Risk Clues (Unaffected)
  • >2 individuals on SAME side with breast cancer
  • At least one ovarian cancer on same side
  • FDR with breast cancer before age 45
  • Known susceptibility gene mutation in family
  • Family member with breast + ovarian combination
  • Male breast cancer in family
  • AJ ancestry in family
Risk Management
🔍 Breast Surveillance (Female)
  • Monthly BSE from age 18
  • Clinical exam every 6 months from age 30
  • Annual mammogram from age 30
  • Annual MRI — days 7–15 of cycle in premenopausal
  • MRI sensitivity: 94% vs mammogram 9%
🔪 Risk-Reducing Surgery
  • RRBM → ↓ breast risk by ~90%
  • RRSO → ↓ ovarian risk 85–95%
  • RRSO ALSO → ↓ breast risk by ~50%
  • RRSO at age 35–40 or after childbearing
  • Fallopian tubes MUST also be removed
  • 1–4% residual risk of peritoneal carcinoma (PPC)
💊 Chemoprevention
  • Tamoxifen: ~50% ↓ contralateral BC risk
  • OCP: ↓ ovarian risk ~50%; slight ↑ BRCA1 breast risk before age 40
  • Ovarian surveillance (not ready for RRSO): TVS + CA-125 q6–12mo from age 35
  • Note: surveillance does NOT reliably detect early ovarian cancer
🚹 Male BRCA Carriers: Monthly BSE + clinical exam. BRCA2: targeted PSA screening. BRCA2 = 8.6× prostate risk + 23× risk for early-onset prostate cancer (<55 yrs).

🔵 12 · Lynch Syndrome (HNPCC) ⭐⭐⭐
🫁

Lynch Syndrome — HNPCC

Genes: MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2 · Autosomal Dominant · MLH1+MSH2 = >90% of cases · DNA Mismatch Repair

Genes
  • MLH1 chr 3p21.3   MSH2 chr 2p21
  • MSH6 chr 2p16   PMS2 chr 7p22
  • MLH1 + MSH2 = >90% of Lynch mutations; MSH6 = ~7%
  • EPCAM deletions → MSH2 silencing (20–25% of MSH2-absent cases)
  • Function: DNA Mismatch Repair (MMR)
  • Characterized by MSI due to defective MMR
Lynch variants: Turcot = Lynch + glioblastoma | Muir-Torre = Lynch + sebaceous skin lesions
Lifetime Cancer Risks
Colorectal Cancer50–80%
Endometrial Cancer40–60%
Gastric Cancer11–19%
Ovarian Cancer9–12%
CRC in Lynch = typically right-sided; diagnosed 10 yrs earlier than sporadic CRC.
Amsterdam Criteria II ⭐ EXAM

Research classification — many Lynch families do NOT meet this. Do not rely on it alone.

  1. At least 2 relatives with HNPCC-associated cancer
  2. One must be a FDR of the other two
  3. At least 2 successive generations affected
  4. At least one tumor diagnosed before age 50
  5. FAP excluded; tumors histologically verified
Bethesda Guidelines ⭐ EXAM

Identifies patients for MSI/IHC testing on already-diagnosed tumors.

  1. CRC patient younger than age 50
  2. Patient has multiple HNPCC tumors
  3. FDR with HNPCC tumor before age 50
  4. At least 2 FDRs or SDRs with HNPCC tumors at any age
  5. Patient <60 yrs with CRC showing MSI features microscopically

🟡 13 · FAP & Colorectal Syndromes ⭐⭐⭐
Classic FAP
  • Gene: APC — chr 5q21-q22
  • Inheritance: AD; ~25% de novo (no family history!)
  • Incidence: 1 in 8,000–15,000
  • Hundreds to THOUSANDS of colorectal adenomas
  • Polyps begin in teenage years
  • Without colectomy: ~100% colon cancer by 4th–5th decade
  • Left-sided CRC (vs Lynch = right-sided)
  • Extra-colonic: CHRPE (eye), osteomas, desmoids, hepatoblastoma, medulloblastoma, papillary thyroid cancer
🧠 "Full of Polyps" → Colectomy is life-saving!
AFAP & MUTYH (MAP)
AFAP — Attenuated FAP
  • <100 adenomas (10–100); smaller and flatter
  • Right-sided colon; mean cancer age 50–52 yrs
  • If APC negative → test for MYH mutations
MUTYH-Associated Polyposis (MAP)
  • Gene: MYH — chr 1p32 · Base-excision repair
  • Inheritance: Autosomal Recessive — ONLY AR colon syndrome!
  • Biallelic mutations: 7–17% of FAP-phenotype patients with no APC mutation
  • Biallelic MYH: cancer risk up to 28× greater than monoallelic
  • Family history often less obvious (recessive)
  • Colonoscopy from age 25–30, every 3–5 years

⚡ 14 · Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia (MEN) ⭐⭐⭐
MEN1
  • Gene: MEN1 — chr 11q13
  • Incidence: 1 in 5,000–50,000 | Penetrance: ~80% by age 50
  • ~10% de novo mutations
  • "3 P's" Tumors:
  • → 🧠 Pituitary tumors
  • → 🦴 Parathyroid tumors → hyperparathyroidism = presenting symptom
  • → 🫁 Pancreatic islet cell tumors (gastrinomas, insulinomas)
  • Also: adrenal, carcinoid, angiofibromas, lipomas
🧠 "3 P's" = Pituitary + Parathyroid + Pancreas. Hyperparathyroidism is first sign!
MEN2 — Three Subtypes
  • Gene: RET proto-oncogene — chr 10q11.2 | Incidence: 1:30,000
SubtypeFeatures
MEN2A
(most common)		Medullary thyroid (100%) + Pheochromocytoma (50%) + Hyperparathyroidism (15–30%)
MEN2B
(most aggressive)		Medullary thyroid <age 10 + Pheo + Mucosal neuromas (eyelids/lips/tongue) + Marfanoid habitus. Thyroidectomy in 1st year of life!
FMTC
(mildest)		Medullary thyroid ONLY. Older onset. 50% penetrance by age 36.
🧠 MEN2B = Youngest + Most aggressive + Marfanoid + Neuromas. Thyroidectomy YEAR 1!
🚩 Red Flags: Pheochromocytoma, paraganglioma, medullary thyroid cancer, parathyroid carcinoma — even WITHOUT family history — warrant comprehensive genetic assessment.

⚠️ 17 · Li-Fraumeni Syndrome (LFS) ⭐⭐⭐
Basics
  • Gene: TP53 (p53) — chr 17p13.1 — "guardian of the genome"
  • Inheritance: AD | Very rare: ~400 families worldwide
  • Penetrance: ~50% by age 30; ~100% by age 70
  • Breast risk = 100× greater than general population
  • Cancer starts in childhood or very young adulthood
  • Multiple primary cancers common in one individual
Cancer Spectrum & Surveillance
  • 🦴 Sarcomas (osteo-, chondro-, rhabdomyo-)
  • 🎗️ Breast cancer (very early onset)
  • 🧠 Brain — glioblastoma
  • 🩸 Leukemia + Lymphoma
  • 🫁 Adrenocortical carcinoma
  • These = ~80% of cancers in LFS families
  • Annual exams from childhood
  • Breast: BSE + CBE q6mo + mammogram + MRI from age 20–25
  • MRI preferred for young patients (avoids radiation)
  • Colonoscopy from age 25, every 2–5 years
🧠 p53 lost = many cancers at VERY early age

⚖️ 19 · Ethics, GINA & Legal Issues ⭐⭐
The Four Ethical Principles
1. Autonomy

Right to self-determination. Includes right to KNOW AND right NOT to know genetic status. Patient's informed decision must be respected even if provider disagrees.

2. Nonmaleficence — "First, Do No Harm"

Primum non nocere. Threat example: uninformative negative result creating false sense of security.

3. Beneficence

Testing is beneficent IF enhanced surveillance/prevention decreases morbidity and mortality. Needs evidence-based management strategies.

4. Justice / Equity

All patients should have equal access to genetic services regardless of financial means. Nurses must be advocates.

Legal Framework
HIPAA (1996)
  • Genetic info cannot be used as a preexisting condition or to determine insurance eligibility
  • Applies only to group and self-funded plans
  • Does NOT protect against rate hikes or individual genetic data access
GINA 2008 ⭐ EXAM
  • ✅ Protects: Health insurance + Employment
  • Health: prohibits using genetic info for medical underwriting
  • Employment: prohibits denying jobs or benefits based on genetic info
  • ❌ Does NOT cover: Life insurance, Disability insurance, Long-term care, Active-duty military
  • Does NOT supersede state laws that are more protective
🧠 GINA = Health insurance + Employment ONLY!
ADA (1995 Guidelines)
  • Definition of "disability" extended to include discrimination based on genetic information
  • These guidelines are NOT law — interpretation of ADA language
  • May be overturned in court
  • Self-insured employers may be exempt from state health insurance laws due to ERISA
Duty to Warn: Clinician informs the proband AND encourages sharing with biological relatives. NOT legally required to directly contact relatives. Right to know may supersede right NOT to know when early intervention can save lives.

📋 20 · Informed Consent for Genetic Testing ⭐⭐⭐
Informed consent PRECEDES genetic testing. Two versions: Chapter 6 (5 elements) and Chapter 10 (15 elements). Both are testable!
5 Core Elements (Ch. 6)
1
Competence — comprehend implications of all results; use of DNA; plans for follow-up and family sharing
2
Disclosure — procedures, risks, benefits; fees; emotional impact; discrimination potential
3
Understanding — risk management options with AND without testing; heritability; test limitations
4
Voluntariness — voluntary, no coercion. Right to informed refusal = equal to informed consent
5
Consent — by the individual or appropriate surrogate decision-maker
15 Elements (Ch. 10)
  • A. Purpose of the genetic test
  • B. Motivation for testing
  • C. Risks and benefits
  • D. Limitations of genetic testing
  • E. Inheritance pattern of the gene(s) tested
  • F. Risk of misidentified paternity
  • G. Accuracy and sensitivity of the test method
  • H. Outcomes of genetic testing
  • I. Confidentiality of results
  • J. Possibility of discrimination
  • K. Alternatives to genetic testing
  • L. How testing impacts healthcare decisions
  • M. Cost of testing
  • N. Right to refuse
  • O. Testing in children — only when clinical utility established

🧠 22 · Psychological & Social Issues ⭐⭐
Psychological Consequences
  • Survivor guilt: observed in those who did NOT inherit the family mutation
  • Transmitter guilt: when family members pass the mutation to their offspring
  • Heightened anxiety: learning about substantially increased cancer risk
  • Depression and anger: may occur regardless of genetic status
  • Personal identity issues: genetic information involves the essence of an individual
  • Regret: for past decisions inconsistent with test results
  • Uncertainty: testing does NOT tell if or when cancer develops; no proven strategy in many cases
Social & Financial Issues
  • Predisposition testing may be expensive; not all insurers cover it
  • Insurers may not cover enhanced surveillance unless efficacy proven
  • Insurers may not cover prophylactic surgery unless benefit proven
  • Small number of trained genetic providers — availability varies
  • Stigmatization: within family and social network
  • Intrafamilial issues:
  • → Coercion regarding testing decisions
  • → Disclosure tensions within families
  • → Effect on partner (not at genetic risk but children may be impacted)

💻 23 · Testing Methods & NGS ⭐
Types of Genetic Testing
  • Sanger sequencing (direct): determines gene sequence; may miss mutations outside coding region or large genomic rearrangements
  • GWAS: surveys entire genome for small nucleotide alterations (SNPs); association studies
  • Microarray: hundreds to thousands of DNA/RNA/protein segments on slides; brighter dot = greater expression
  • LGRs: detect large genomic rearrangements, deletions, duplications
  • WES — Whole Exome Sequencing: sequences protein-coding regions (exons) only
  • WGS — Whole Genome Sequencing: entire genome
  • Direct-to-consumer (23andMe): FDA approved for BRCA1/2 BUT only includes 3 AJ founder mutations — NOT for clinical decision making
NGS Clinical Implications
  • Faster and less costly than traditional Sanger sequencing
  • Enables multi-gene panel testing simultaneously
  • Detects somatic cancer genome alterations (substitutions, insertions, deletions, copy number variations)
  • Increases secondary findings and incidental findings
  • Leads to more VUS classifications requiring follow-up
  • Must discuss incidental findings with patients BEFORE testing
  • As costs decrease → more full genome sequencing → more unexpected results
🧠 More NGS = More VUS + More incidental findings = More pre-test counseling needed!

📋 SCREENING & EARLY DETECTION GUIDELINES — Chapter 2 ⭐⭐⭐
Key Organizations: ACS = American Cancer Society | NCCN = National Comprehensive Cancer Network | USPSTF = U.S. Preventive Services Task Force
Levels of Prevention
Primary Prevention
  • Reduce risk factors OR increase individual's resistance
  • Most effective method
  • Goal: limit the incidence of cancer
  • Examples: vaccination (HPV), smoking cessation, diet modification
Secondary Prevention
  • Early detection and treatment of asymptomatic individuals through screening
  • Goal: prevent disease progression
  • Examples: mammography, colonoscopy, Pap smear, PSA
Tertiary Prevention
  • Application of effective therapy to improve outcomes
  • Decrease morbidity and mortality in affected individuals
  • Clinical problem-solving in symptomatic patients

🔵 TABLE 2.4 — Prostate Cancer Screening Recommendations
PSA = a glycoprotein produced by prostatic epithelial cells; the ONLY tumor marker currently used in prostate cancer screening. Limited sensitivity and specificity. PSA levels affected by age, BPH, inflammation, urethral/prostatic trauma, ejaculation within 48 hours, and medications.
OrganizationPopulationTest & Schedule

High risk: African American men OR men with FDR diagnosed with prostate cancer before age 65 Discussion at age 45

NCCN, 2017 Age 45–75 Begin risk–benefit discussion about DRE and PSA. PSA <1.0 ng/mL → repeat every 2–4 yr | PSA 1–3 ng/mL → repeat every 1–2 yr | PSA >3 ng/mL or suspicious DRE → repeat PSA, consider biopsy
Age >75 Testing with caution — only in very healthy men with little or no comorbidity
USPSTF, 2012 All men Recommends NO routine screening for prostate cancer
⚠️ DRE should NOT be used as a stand-alone test — used with PSA. PSA screening has NOT correlated with decreases in PC mortality and has instead prompted aggressive therapies with significant side effects.
🫁 TABLE 2.5 — Lung Cancer Screening Guidelines
LDCT (Low-Dose Computed Tomography) = most sensitive test available for lung cancer screening. 20% reduction in lung cancer mortality demonstrated by National Lung Screening Trial (LDCT vs plain CXR in 53,454 high-risk participants). Chest X-ray (CXR) and sputum cytology = NO RCT evidence of benefit.
OrganizationPopulationTest & Schedule
ACS, 2013 Age 55–74 at high risk: ≥30 pack-year smoking history, currently smoking OR quit within 15 years Low-dose LDCT — after informed shared decision making regarding limitations and benefits. Smoking cessation counseling remains a priority.
NCCN, 2018 High-risk: Age 55–74 + ≥30 pack-year history + quit within 15 yr; OR Age ≥50 + ≥20 pack-year + ≥1 additional risk factor increasing lung cancer risk to ≥1.3% LDCT screening recommended
Moderate-risk: Age ≥50 + ≥20 pack-year history or secondhand exposure Lung cancer screening is NOT recommended
Low-risk: Age <50 OR <20 pack-year history Lung cancer screening is NOT recommended
USPSTF, 2013 Age 55–80 with ≥30 pack-year history, currently smoking OR quit within 15 years Annual LDCT in persons who can undergo surgical resection if tumor is discovered
Screening not recommended for general population. Consider screening for ages 55–80 with ≥30 pack-year smoking history who either continue to smoke or have quit less than 15 years ago. ~95% false-positive results occurred in lung cancer screening trials.
