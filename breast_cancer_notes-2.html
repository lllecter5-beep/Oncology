Global & US Statistics

  • Globally: most common cancer in women — 1.7 million new cases in 2012
  • Second leading cause of cancer deaths in women (after lung cancer) in developed countries; number one in developing countries
  • US 2018: ~268,670 new cases · ~41,400 deaths (40,920 female + 480 male)
  • 30% of all new cancer cases in women
  • Death rate peaked at 33.2/100,000 in 1989 → declined 39% by 2015 (322,600 fewer deaths)
  • Between 2002–2003: 7% drop in incidence due to ↓ HRT use

Male Breast Cancer

  • 100× less common than female BC
  • US 2018: 2,550 men diagnosed; 480 deaths
  • Black men: 2.7/100,000 vs. white men: 1.9/100,000
  • Average age at diagnosis: 65 years
  • 5-year survival (all stages): 84% — Stage 0&I: 100% · Stage II: 91% · Stage III: 72% · Stage IV: 20%
  • All men diagnosed with BC should undergo multigene genetic testing (NCCN)

02

Anatomy & Physiology

Breast Structure

  • Overlies chest (pectoral) muscles; lies between sternum and midaxillary line, 2nd–6th ribs, below clavicle
  • Connective tissue (collagen + elastin)
  • Nerves: T4 thoracic spinal nerve → innervates nipple and areola
  • Blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, fat, lobules, ducts
  • Suspensory Cooper's ligaments
  • Nipple and areola complex

Functional vs. Anatomic Breast

  • Glandular tissue: 14–18 lactiferous lobes → TDLUs (2.0–4.55mm ducts) → produce fatty breast milk
  • Adipose ratio: 2:1 (glands:fat) in lactating; 1:1 in non-lactating; fat ↑ post-menopause
  • Size and shape vary woman to woman; change throughout life
  • Men may develop breast tissue = gynecomastia

Lymphatic Drainage — Clinically Critical

  • 75% of lymph fluid → axilla (pectoral, subscapular, humeral nodes — Levels I, II, III)
  • 25% → parasternal nodes, contralateral breast, or abdomen
  • Also: infraclavicular and supraclavicular nodes
  • Internal mammary nodes involved in ~25% of upper inner quadrant tumors; 15% of outer quadrant
  • Axillary node status = single most important prognostic indicator
  • ⚠️ Exam Favorite: السؤال المتكرر — "أهم عامل تنبؤي (prognostic) في سرطان الثدي؟" الجواب: Axillary lymph node status.

Risk Factors

Key Concept: 90–95% of breast cancers have NO single identifiable cause. Only 5–10% are directly linked to BRCA1/2. Female gender and increasing age are the two most important overall risk factors. A person can have multiple risk factors and never develop BC — or develop it with no known risk factors.

04

Genetics & Hereditary Syndromes⭐⭐⭐

BRCA1 & BRCA2 Basics

  • BRCA1: chromosome 17q21
  • BRCA2: chromosome 13q12.3
  • Both: tumor suppressor genes — mutation → DNA damage not repaired → cancer
  • Autosomal dominant; more common in Ashkenazi Jewish women
  • BRCA1 lifetime breast risk: 55–85%
  • BRCA2 lifetime breast risk: 45–85%
  • BRCA1 ovarian risk: 39–46%; BRCA2: 11–17%
  • BRCA1 → often TNBC (basal-like); BRCA2 → often ER+ adenocarcinoma
  • Bilateral prophylactic oophorectomy → ~80% ↓ ovarian/fallopian/peritoneal cancer

Male BC Genetics

  • 4–40% of male BC have a genetic abnormality
  • 1 in 5 men with BC has a family history
  • BRCA1: 1/100 risk; BRCA2: 6/100 risk
  • Klinefelter syndrome (extra X chromosome) → ↑ estrogen → ↑ BC risk
  • All men diagnosed with BC must undergo multigene genetic testing
Indications for Genetic Testing Referral
  • BC before age 50 · TNBC any age · Ovarian cancer any age
  • Male BC · ≥2 primary BCs · BC + ovarian cancer in same person
  • Pancreatic or high-grade prostate cancer
  • Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry
  • FDR with BC before age 45

Risk Management for BRCA Carriers

Surveillance
  • Monthly self-breast awareness from age 18
  • Clinical exam every 6 months from age 30
  • Annual mammography from age 30
  • Annual MRI days 7–15 of cycle (premenopausal)
  • MRI sensitivity: 94% vs. mammography 9% in BRCA carriers
Risk-Reducing Surgery
  • RRBM (Risk-Reducing Bilateral Mastectomy) → ↓ breast risk ~80–90%
  • RRSO (Risk-Reducing Salpingo-Oophorectomy) → ↓ ovarian risk 80–85%; also ↓ breast risk ~50%
  • RRSO recommended: age 35–40 or after childbearing
  • Residual risk: 1–4% Primary Peritoneal Carcinoma (PPC)
  • Tamoxifen: 44–50% ↓ contralateral BC in BRCA carriers

06

Prevention

Primary · Chemoprevention · Surgical

Primary Prevention — Lifestyle Modifications

  • Healthy weight: obesity after menopause → ↑ estrogen in fat tissue → ↑ risk. Weight loss + healthy BMI ↓ risk
  • Physical activity: ≥150 min/week moderate-vigorous exercise → ↓ hormone levels + ↑ immune function → ↓ risk 20–80%
  • Alcohol: <1 drink/day. Strongest modifiable risk factor. 3–4 drinks/day = 30% ↑ risk
  • Smoking cessation: especially important in premenopausal women
  • Diet: ↑ fruits, vegetables, fiber, low-fat dairy. Limit red meat, processed foods, high sugar. Monounsaturated fats (olive oil) may be protective
  • Stress reduction: mindfulness and psychological support (indirect link to risk)
Protective Factors: Breastfeeding (long duration) · First pregnancy before age 20 · Higher number of births · Regular physical exercise · Healthy body weight throughout life

FDA Approved ONLY: Tamoxifen and Raloxifene. Despite strong data, uptake remains much lower than expected — nurses must educate patients that the risk of endometrial cancer with tamoxifen is only ~3–4 per 1000 women (less than 1%).

Bilateral Prophylactic Mastectomy (RRBM)

  • ↓ BC risk >90% — indicated for BRCA mutations or very strong family history
  • Removes most breast tissue
  • Psychological impact, loss of sensation, body image changes must be addressed
  • Studies show women chose it for risk reduction and peace of mind — not necessarily for improved survival

Bilateral Salpingo-Oophorectomy (RRSO)

  • Recommended for women with BRCA mutations
  • ↓ ovarian cancer risk ~80–85% · ↓ breast cancer risk ~50%
  • Recommended age: 35–40 or after childbearing completed
  • Causes immediate menopause → cardiovascular and osteoporosis risk must be managed carefully
  • Residual risk: 1–4% Primary Peritoneal Carcinoma (PPC)

Mammography

  • Standard primary screening tool
  • 3D Tomosynthesis → ↓ false positives, ↓ recall rates
  • Digital mammography: NOT shown superior in RCTs over traditional

Ultrasonography

  • Supplement for dense breasts
  • Differentiates solid vs. cystic
  • Does NOT replace mammography

MRI Breast

  • Sensitivity: ~94%
  • BRCA+ surveillance; staging; response assessment
  • Best days 7–15 of cycle (premenopausal)
Breast Self-Awareness (BSA): BSE as a formal monthly exam is no longer officially recommended by major guidelines due to lack of evidence in mortality reduction and high false-positive rates. However, women should be familiar with how their breasts normally feel and report any changes promptly. CBE: every 3 years (age 20–39), then annually from age 40. High-risk women: twice-yearly CBE. Symptoms requiring immediate evaluation: new lump, nipple discharge (especially bloody), skin changes (peau d'orange), nipple inversion, unexplained pain.

Pathogenesis & Premalignant Lesions

Mutation Accumulation · Tumor Heterogeneity · ADH · ALH

How Breast Cancer Develops

  • Starts with a single cell that accumulates multiple genetic mutations → learns to proliferate rapidly without normal controls
  • Each tumor = heterogeneous cell population — each cell has different proliferation rate, metastatic potential, and treatment sensitivity
  • "Fittest" cells survive therapy → drives treatment resistance and recurrence
  • Benign lesions: Nonproliferative (simple cysts) = NOT a BC risk factor. Proliferative lesions with atypia = significantly ↑ risk of future invasive BC
FeatureADH (Atypical Ductal Hyperplasia)ALH (Atypical Lobular Hyperplasia)
LocationBreast ductsBreast lobules
Risk increase4-fold ↑ relative risk of BC development
Ipsilateral risk2× higher ipsilateral risk for 5 years; then both breasts assume equal risk
Cancer typeEpithelial ductal cancer (moderate-high grade; usually ER+)Lobular cancer pattern
ManagementComplete removal → confirm absence of malignancy → close follow-up + chemoprevention (tamoxifen, raloxifene, or AIs) + stop exogenous estrogens
Important caveatRemoving atypical tissue ≠ removing risk — biopsy bed may contain embedded occult BC cells

ER/PR Receptors

  • 73% of invasive BC are ER+; 70% are PR+
  • Receptors: proteins inside cells that bind estrogen/progesterone → stimulate cell proliferation and DNA replication → mutations → cancer
  • Receptor status: determined from biopsy — largest piece of tumor dictates final result
  • ⚠️ Exam Favorite: Luminal A = أفضل إنذار (best prognosis) · Triple-Negative (TNBC) = أسوأ إنذار ولا خيارات علاج موجّه. وBRCA1 → عادةً TNBC.

HER2/neu Receptor

  • Tyrosine kinase family; chromosome 17
  • 20–30% of invasive BC are HER2+; 70% are HER2−
  • HER2+ → ↑ recurrence risk, poorer prognosis
  • Testing: IHC → if equivocal (2+) → FISH (more sensitive; FISH result is final)
  • Targeted therapy: trastuzumab and/or pertuzumab

⚠️ HER2-Positivity vs HER2-Enriched — Don't Confuse:
HER2-positivity (rate ~20–30% of all BC) ≠ HER2-enriched molecular subtype (prevalence ~10–15%).
• HER2-enriched subtype = HER2+ AND ER/PR negative
• Luminal B (HER2+) subtype = HER2+ AND ER/PR positive
Both are HER2-positive tumors but classified as different molecular subtypes.

Clinical Manifestations

Symptoms · Physical Examination Findings

Symptoms (Patient-Reported)

  • Painless lump or thickening in breast or axilla — most common first symptom
  • Change in breast size, shape, or contour
  • Nipple discharge (especially bloody or unilateral)
  • Nipple inversion or retraction
  • Breast warmth and diffuse swelling (IBC hallmark)
  • Unexplained breast or nipple pain
  • Most BC = palpable mass WITHOUT other findings
  • Malignant lesions are rarely tender

Physical Examination Findings

  • Skin dimpling / retraction — invasion of Cooper suspensory ligaments + fixation to chest wall
  • Peau d'orange ("skin of the orange") — dermal lymphatic blockage; characteristic of malignant/IBC disease
  • Nipple retraction or elevation — tumor fixation or infiltration into underlying tissues
  • Nipple discharge or erosion
  • Heat and erythema — inflammation OR sign of IBC
  • Axillary / supraclavicular lymphadenopathy — palpable nodes
  • Most common location: upper outer quadrant (adenocarcinoma)

Diagnostic Workup

History · Imaging · Biopsy · Molecular Tests · Staging Workup

Patient History — Key Elements

  • Previous BC, biopsies, surgeries, radiation exposure, other cancers
  • Age at menarche, menopause, pregnancies, breastfeeding
  • HRT or OCP use (type, route, frequency)
  • Surgery to ovaries or uterus
  • Family history: type, age at onset, lineage (min. 1st and 2nd degree — updated annually)
  • Known BRCA mutation status
  • Lifestyle: smoking, alcohol, diet, exercise, occupational exposures
  • Prior mammographic screening results

Staging / Metastatic Work-up (if cancer confirmed)

  • Abdominal ultrasound → liver metastases

Pembrolizumab (Keytruda)
  • PD-1 inhibitor — blocks immune checkpoint → allows T-cells to attack cancer
  • KEYNOTE-522: Early/locally advanced TNBC — current standard of care (neoadjuvant + adjuvant)
  • Also: metastatic TNBC if PD-L1 positive
  • SE: Immune-related adverse events: colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, endocrinopathies, skin reactions, fatigue, infusion reactions
Bevacizumab (Avastin)
  • Anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody → prevents angiogenesis
  • Historically used for metastatic HER2− BC with paclitaxel or capecitabine

  • Contraindicated: within 28 days of surgery; uncontrolled hypertension; metastatic brain lesions

G — Bone-Modifying Agents

Zoledronic Acid (Bisphosphonate)
  • Inhibits osteoclast activity → ↓ bone resorption → ↓ bone pain, fractures, hypercalcemia
  • IV infusion over 15–30 minutes, once a month
  • Used: bone metastases; adjuvant in postmenopausal women (↓ bone recurrence); prevention of bone loss on AIs
  • Therapy continues for LIFE regardless of other therapy changes
  • SE: Acute phase reaction (fever + bone pain after 1st dose); hypocalcemia; renal toxicity; Osteonecrosis of Jaw (ONJ) — rare but serious

Denosumab (Xgeva)
  • RANK-ligand (RANKL) inhibitor — prevents osteoclast maturation
  • Subcutaneous injection; used for bone metastases prevention and treatment
  • SE: Hypocalcemia; ONJ; atypical femur fractures
  • Does not require dose adjustment for renal impairment (unlike bisphosphonates)
  • Monitor serum calcium during therapy

Nursing Care

Pre-op · Post-op · Lymphedema · Radiation · Chemo · Psychosocial · Survivorship

Pre-Operative & Post-Operative Care

Pre-Op
  • Assess anxiety level — pre-surgical anxiety ↑ biophysical stress → may impede healing
  • Educate about procedure, expected outcomes, reconstruction options
  • Discuss body image concerns before surgery
  • Ensure informed consent and patient participation in decision-making
Post-Mastectomy
  • Wound care + maintain functioning wound drains
  • Blocked drains → seroma/hematoma → infection → flap necrosis
  • Exercises begin as early as 24 hours post-op
  • Affected arm in adducted position; limited wrist and elbow exercises
  • Flex fingers; touch hand to shoulder; NO ball squeezing — ↑ blood flow → ↑ swelling
  • Educate about altered sensations: numbness, tingling, phantom breast sensation — may persist indefinitely

Lymphedema Care

  • No BP cuffs, blood draws, or injections in affected arm
  • Teach arm circumference measurement — notify provider if increases
  • Compression therapy and manual lymphatic drainage
  • Protect arm from injury and infection
  • Monitor for cellulitis
  • Refer to lymphedema therapist

Chemotherapy Nursing Care

  • Monitor CBC and vital signs before each cycle
  • Manage nausea/vomiting with antiemetics
  • Encourage adequate hydration and nutrition
  • Educate about infection prevention (hand hygiene, avoid crowds)
  • Provide psychological support for hair loss and fatigue
  • Monitor for peripheral neuropathy (taxanes) and cardiotoxicity (anthracyclines)
  • Trastuzumab: monitor cardiac function (ECHO or MUGA) every 3 months. Educate to report SOB or chest pain

Distress Management

  • Patients report: vulnerability, loss of control, uncertainty, stress, loss of energy
  • Family support may be inadequate — family also experiencing emotional distress
  • Anxiety affects up to 30% of cancer patients
  • Pre-surgical anxiety → ↑ biophysical stress → may impede healing and recovery
  • Anxiety does NOT resolve quickly after surgery
  • Screen: PHQ-9 (depression), GAD-7 (anxiety), Distress Thermometer
  • Provide interventions, referrals; help patients regain sense of control
  • Comprehensive survivorship care + episodic psychosocial needs assessment throughout

Body Image & Self-Image

  • Multiple self-image challenges before AND after surgery
  • Issues: breast loss/defect, tissue damage, lymphedema, seromas, altered muscle definition, changed ROM, breast tenderness
  • Chemo adds: alopecia, weight changes, fatigue
  • Discuss myths and concerns openly with patient and family
  • Refer to psychology if issues persist beyond early diagnostic period
  • Discuss reconstruction options and external prostheses

Patient Education (Core Curriculum Ch. 13)

  • Explain disease and treatment at patient's level — nonjudgmental
  • Encourage discussion of physical AND emotional changes
  • Explore personal values and beliefs related to treatment choices
  • Facilitate patient's involvement in decision-making to extent desired
  • Educate about lymphedema risk; teach arm circumference measurement
  • Arm precautions: prevent trauma and infection
  • Inform about altered sensations: numbness, tingling, phantom breast — may persist indefinitely
  • Assess for menopausal symptoms from endocrine therapy or chemo-induced ovarian failure
  • Monitor and manage all treatment side effects
  • Teach self-breast awareness and axillary self-examination
  • Address fertility and pregnancy after treatment (younger patients)

Survivorship Care Plan

  • Create survivorship care plans — tertiary prevention focus
  • Address: smoking cessation, weight control, diet, exercise, stress management (Healthy People 2020)
  • These topics are rarely discussed in daily oncology practice but are critical tertiary prevention strategies
  • Assess: coping, support system, body image, sexual identity, role relationships
  • Provide information on community resources, support groups, rehabilitation, breast prostheses
  • Annual surveillance schedule: gynecology (tamoxifen users), mammography, bone density (AI users), cardiac monitoring (HER2-targeted therapy users)
