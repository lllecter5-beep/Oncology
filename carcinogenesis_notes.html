⚠️ Key concept: Carcinogenesis is a complex, multistep process. Each cancer is different due to heterogeneity created from diverse cell types plus genetic/epigenetic differences — put simply, each cancer is different.
🔬 01 · Cancer Development Models ⭐⭐⭐
Clonal Evolution Model
  • Initial DNA damage → benign tumor growth
  • Over time, accumulation of driver and passenger mutations → increasingly malignant characteristics
  • Cells with most advantageous mutations proliferate faster (survival of the fittest)
  • Late-stage tumors show significant genetic heterogeneity
  • Clonal expansion = process of cells accumulating genetic changes over time (different from one cell to the next)
🧠 Each tumor has a unique set of mutated genes — like a "snowflake." No two cancers are exactly alike.
Cancer Stem Cell (CSC) Model
  • Small subpopulation of mutationally corrupt heterogeneous cancer stem cells drives tumor growth
  • Only one subpopulation has tumorigenic capability; others demonstrate limited proliferation potential
  • CSCs self-renew mitotically — at least one progeny maintains ability to multiply virtually forever
  • CSCs in G₀ (resting) phase are most resistant to treatment and resistant to cancer treatment
  • Non-CSCs in G₁, S, G₂, M phases are dividing rapidly → can be destroyed by treatment
  • Resting CSCs can re-enter active phases later → continuous proliferation or re-emergence after remission
⚠️ CSCs in G₀ are why cancers can recur after apparent remission — they are NOT in active phases during treatment.
Plasticity Model (CSC)
  • Plasticity exists between non-CSCs (low tumorigenic) and CSCs (high tumorigenic)
  • CSCs move in a one-way fashion: either replenish CSC pool or become non-CSCs with very low tumorigenic potential
  • New research: some carcinomas can convert non-CSCs into aggressive de novo CSCs → bidirectional conversion
  • Explains resistance to chemotherapy after long-term remission/latency
Inflammation Theory
  • Infectious agents + their relationships with inflammatory cells + chronic inflammation = primary cause
  • Includes proliferation, survival, and migration of cancer cells
  • Factors from innate immune system: selectins, chemokines (NF-κB), and their receptors encourage invasion, migration, and metastasis
3 Connective Tissue Classes Supporting CSCs
Class 1
  • Endothelial cells, pericytes, perivascular cells
  • Organize CSCs into vascular niches within primary tumors and metastatic sites
Class 2 — CAFs
  • Cancer-associated fibroblasts
  • Switch to unusual aerobic form of glycolysis
  • Secrete lactate + pyruvate as energy to fuel cancer cell proliferation
Class 3
  • Columnar cells
  • Important for water absorption

🧪 02 · Causes & Types of Mutations ⭐⭐
Environmental Carcinogens
  • Tobacco, alcohol
  • Sun exposure & sunbeds, ionizing radiation
  • Occupational hazards
  • Indoor and outdoor air pollution
  • Processed/red meat, lack of fiber/vegetables
  • Hyperglycemia/diabetes, overweight
  • Lack of physical exercise, post-menopause
  • Infections (viral, bacterial, parasitic)
  • Reproductive factors
Types of DNA Changes
  • Point mutations (single base pair change)
  • Deletions, duplications, insertions
  • Chromosomal translocations
  • Chromosomal aberrations
  • Incorporation of viral genetic material
  • Epigenetic inactivation
  • Loss of Heterozygosity (LOH)
Somatic (Noninherited) Mutations
  • Most common type
  • Found in DNA of every cell except sperm and egg
  • Caused by environmental carcinogens
  • Not passed to offspring
  • Also called somatic mutations
Germline (Inherited) Mutations
  • Inherited DNA from egg or sperm
  • Currently 853 known germline variants contributing to cancer development
  • BRCA1, BRCA2 are examples
  • Inheriting one variant → more susceptible to certain types of cancer
  • Transmitted in autosomal dominant fashion (e.g., TP53 germline = Li-Fraumeni syndrome)
Driver vs Passenger Mutations
Driver Mutations
  • Directly associated with oncogenesis (e.g., TP53)
  • Selected in the microenvironment for clonal advantage
  • Allow thousands of additional mutations to occur
  • Can be targeted for treatment individually or in staggered approach
Passenger Mutations
  • Found in same tumors but do NOT contribute to cancer development
  • Viewed as "along for the ride"
  • Different cancers may have variable mix of driver and passenger mutations yet still demonstrate presence of similar phenotypes

Loss of Heterozygosity (LOH)
Homozygous = both alleles identical. Heterozygous = if one allele has inherited mutation of tumor suppressor gene (with loss of genetic material). When remaining allele is also mutated → gene and protein lose normal functioning. This further alteration and loss of genetic material = LOH. Includes: loss of entire chromosome, translocation, reduplication of chromosome piece with abnormal gene, or development of point mutation in second functioning allele.

🔴 Key Oncogenes
RAS Family (KRAS, HRAS, NRAS)
  • Actin cytoskeletal integrity, cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis
  • Produces K-Ras protein → regulates cell division via intracellular RAS/MAPK signaling
  • Point mutation changes proto-oncogene to oncogene
  • Mutated Ras = inactivates negative feedback mechanism → cells keep proliferating
🎯 Associated: Pancreatic cancer, colorectal cancer. Most prevalent mutation in human cancers.
BRAF
  • Produces protein for RAS/MAPK pathway
  • Regulates proliferation, differentiation, migration, apoptosis
  • Most commonly mutated BRAF protein = V600E
  • Especially active in giant congenital melanocytic nevus (GCMN)
  • Somatic missense mutations found in 66% of malignant melanomas
🎯 Associated: Melanoma, colon, rectum, ovarian, thyroid cancer.
MYC
  • Regulatory gene coding for transcription factor
  • Regulates global chromatin structure via histone acetylation
  • Important for cell cycle progression, apoptosis, cellular transformation
  • MYC proteins function as transcription factors to regulate specific target genes
  • Overexpression of mutated Myc gene frequently associated with hematopoietic tumors
🎯 Associated: Burkitt's lymphoma, hematopoietic tumors, leukemias, lymphomas.
BCL2
  • Helps regulate apoptosis (anti-apoptosis)
  • Mitochondrial membrane protein that blocks apoptosis in cells such as lymphocytes
  • Continued proliferation due to BCL2 expression → difficult for follicular lymphoma cells to be destroyed
🎯 Associated: CLL, B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma, breast cancer, follicular lymphoma.
HER2/neu
  • Epidermal growth factor receptor
  • Part of tyrosine kinase receptor family
🎯 Associated: Breast cancer.
🔵 Key Tumor Suppressor Genes
TP53 — "Guardian of the Genome"
  • Located on 17p13
  • Conserves genomic stability and apoptosis
  • Inhibits angiogenesis; activates DNA repair proteins
  • Involved in approximately 50% of sporadic human cancers
  • When inherited as germline mutation → autosomal dominant → hereditary cancer syndromes (Li-Fraumeni syndrome)
  • Acts as first line of defense against glucose uptake by preventing GLUT1 expression
  • Loss of p53 sensitizes tumor cells to metabolic drugs (e.g., metformin)
  • Located in nucleus; binds directly to DNA when damage needs repair; signals apoptosis when unrepairable DNA damage identified
🎯 Associated: Colon, breast, lung cancer; leukemias, lymphomas, sarcomas; Li-Fraumeni syndrome.
PTEN
  • Protein tyrosine phosphatase family
  • Produces ubiquitous protein acting as tumor suppressor
  • Helps regulate cell-cycle division — issues "stop" order to prevent progression through G₁ and triggers apoptosis
  • Participates in migration, adhesion, and angiogenesis
  • Antagonizes PI3K signaling and negatively regulates MAPK pathway → constraints on cell propagation
🎯 Associated: Cowden syndrome, breast, thyroid, head/neck, glioma type 2, prostate, endometrial cancer.
BRCA1/2
  • DNA repair, transcription, and ubiquitination
  • When one germline variant is inherited → more susceptible to cancer
  • Currently 853 known germline variants — BRCA1/2 are well-known examples
🎯 Associated: Breast, ovarian, prostate, melanoma, pancreatic cancer.
RB (Retinoblastoma Gene)
  • Normally inhibits cell division
  • pRB has multiple actions: (1) tumor suppressor for cell growth, (2) inhibition of DNA replication, (3) interaction with proteins to promote apoptosis, (4) promotion of cellular differentiation
🎯 Associated: Childhood retinoblastoma, lung, breast, bone cancers.
CDKN2A (p16 INK4)
  • Cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A
  • Produces two major tumor suppressor proteins: p16 (INK4, INK4A) and p14 (ARF)
  • Regulate p53 cell-cycle regulatory pathways → cell-cycle arrest in G₁ and G₂/M
  • p14 (ARF) produces similar effects in retinoblastoma pathway (RB1)
  • Mutations → promote movement through G₁ and G₂/M → cell proliferation
🎯 Associated: Pancreatic cancer, gastrinomas. p16 mutations → leukemias, bladder cancer, melanoma.
APC
  • Acts as a tumor suppressor
  • Helps control cell division, attachment to other cells in tissue, and whether a cell moves within or away from tissue
🎯 Associated: Colon cancer, familial adenomatous polyposis syndrome.
MEN1
  • Role in repression of telomerase expression
  • Chromatin regulation
🎯 Associated: Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1: parathyroid cancer, carcinoid tumors, pancreatic cancer, pituitary tumor.
🟠 Receptor Tyrosine Kinase / Proto-oncogene
EGFR — RTK / Proto-oncogene
  • Classification: RTK / Proto-oncogene
  • Mutation → uncontrolled proliferation
  • Associated with NSCLC (Non-small cell lung cancer)
📝 Targeted: EGFR inhibitors (e.g., cetuximab, erlotinib)
🟢 DNA Repair / Caretaker Genes
⚠️ Common confusion: MMR genes are sometimes listed under oncogenes in tables — this is incorrect for exam purposes. MMR genes are DNA Repair / Caretaker genes. Their mutation does NOT directly cause cancer but allows mutations to accumulate.
MMR Genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2)
  • Classification: DNA Repair / Caretaker Genes
  • Maintain fidelity of DNA during replication
  • Defects → Microsatellite Instability (MSI)
  • MSI → Lynch Syndrome / HNPCC (Hereditary Nonpolyposis Colon Cancer)
🎯 Associated: Colorectal cancer, endometrial cancer, ovarian cancer.
🧠 MSI = variation in the number of tandem repeated nucleotides. Normal cells have consistent length; cancer cells show variable length.

🔧 04 · DNA Repair Systems ⭐⭐
Most normal cells have driver and passenger mutations that could be restored to their pre-damage levels through normal DNA repair mechanisms — this is obviously important for cancer-free survival. Approximately 130 genes have been linked to DNA repair.
Nucleotide Excision Repair (NER)
  • Addresses mutations associated with xeroderma pigmentosum
Mismatch Repair (MMR)
  • MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2
  • Accompanies inherited predisposition to colorectal cancer (CRC)
  • Defects → microsatellite instability (MSI)
DNA Crosslink Repair
  • Fanconi anemia genes
  • Repairs crosslinked DNA strands
BRCA1/2 Repair
  • Well-known DNA repair genes
  • Associated with breast and ovarian cancer predisposition
⚠️ Mutator Gene Phenotype: Collects increasing numbers of mutated genes left unrepaired due to poor proofreading or incorrect nucleotide insertion. Efficient at acquiring both clonal and random (driver) mutations → allow thousands of mutations to occur. Normal repair mechanisms overlook the DNA damage → incorrect DNA sequences → increased probabilities of mutations and growth advantage.

🔏 05 · Epigenetic Mechanisms ⭐⭐⭐
Definition: Epigenetics describes a mechanism that may change the activity of a gene WITHOUT changing the sequence of DNA. Epigenetic changes occur "above and over" the DNA — they affect the capacity to determine when and where genes might be expressed (turned on) by altering the chromatin. These changes can be reversed, and may be transmitted to daughter cells.
DNA Methylation
  • Adding or removing methyl groups from DNA affects transcription of genes
  • May be affected by diet, environment, and certain medications
  • A frequent cancer finding: methyl groups added where clusters of guanine (G) follow cytosine (C) → CpG islands → gene expression suppressed
  • CpG islands normally occur in areas where gene expression is initiated (promoter)
  • In healthy cells: mostly unmethylated → allowing tumor suppressor genes and proto-oncogenes to be expressed normally → permitting apoptosis and controlled limited cell proliferation
Hypermethylation → silences tumor suppressor genes → loss of apoptosis control. Hypomethylation → overexpression of oncogenes → easier for genes to be overexpressed.
💊 Clinical example: Decitabine hypomethylates DNA → used in treatment of myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). Also: CML, GI stromal tumors, breast cancer.
Histone Modification
  • Chromatin = protein + DNA within a chromosome. Major proteins = histones
  • Histones compact primary DNA by wrapping it tightly (like thread on a spool)
  • Nucleosome = 8 histone molecules with 2 loops of DNA wrapped around each group
  • Euchromatin: loosely packed → active genes (transcription)
  • Heterochromatin: tightly packed → inactive genes (transcription prevented)
  • Both DNA and histone proteins may become modified with addition/removal of methyl, acetyl, phosphate, or ubiquitin groups
  • No chemical group additions = tightly packed histones → no epigenetic reading → prevents DNA expression
  • Chemical group attachment to tail = loose packing → promotes epigenetic reading and DNA expression with protein growth
🧠 Epigenetic readers promote addition/release of methyl, acetyl, and lysine methyl chemical groups to the "tail" — they are used in transcription.

  • Approximately 18–25 nucleotides in length; regulate expression of variety of genes
  • Many deregulated in cancer → have specific roles in various tumor phenotypes → attractive targets for personalized medicine
  • Upregulated: miR-17, miR-21 → colon, lung, stomach, prostate, pancreatic tumors
  • miR-155 upregulated in breast, lung, colon cancers
  • Downregulated: miR-15a, miR-16-1 → chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), prostate tumors, pituitary adenomas
  • Upregulated miRNAs act as oncogenes; lost miRNAs act as tumor suppressors
  • miR-20a: ability to promote senescence induction through downregulation of lymphoma-related factor (LRF/ZBTB7A)

⚙️ 06 · Enabling Factors ⭐⭐
Genomic Instability
  • Cancer cells have defects in mechanisms that regulate genome replication and chromosomal segregation
  • Results in increased rate of genetic alterations compared with normal cells
  • Genetic makeup of cancer cells is less stable than normal cells
HNPCC (Hereditary Nonpolyposis Colon Cancer Syndrome): Characterized by Microsatellite Instability (MSI). Microsatellites = series of tandem repeated nucleotides. Variation in number of tandem repeated nucleotides can be identified in pathology. Normal cells = consistent length; cancer cells = variable length.
Inflammation as an Enabling Factor
  • Inflammatory cells supply signaling molecules to tumor microenvironment: growth factors, survival factors, proangiogenic factors, ECM-modifying enzymes
  • Inductive signals lead to activation of EMT and other hallmark-promoting programs
  • Inflammatory cells release mutagenic agents (reactive oxygen species — ROS) → encourage genetic evolution of cancer cells toward malignancy
  • TNF (Tumor Necrosis Factor): cytokine released by white blood cells in response to inflammation. May have antitumor effect (immune surveillance) but also plays role in carcinogenesis
  • When p53 is mutated → promotes TNFα signaling → activation of macrophages and regulation of immune cells → affects NF-κB (alters DNA transcription control) and JNK (diminishes apoptosis)

⭐ 07 · Hallmarks of Cancer ⭐⭐⭐
Definition: Hallmarks are the traits or characteristics of tumor cells that sustain oncogenic tasks — the biologic capabilities a cancer cell acquires in a progressive multistep process that transforms a normal cell to a malignant cell. Tumors consist of cancer cells with various mutations and supportive tissues (tumor microenvironment) that interact and evolve together.
🔴 A · Sustaining Proliferative Signaling
  • Normal cells regulate proliferation with growth-promoting signals that start and stop mitosis
  • Cancer cells deregulate signals through: (a) production of growth factors themselves, (b) transmission of signals to stimulate normal cells to supply cancer cells with growth factors, (c) presence of mutations creating proto-oncogenes, inactivating tumor suppressor genes, or disrupting negative feedback signaling
  • Mutated Ras oncoprotein: normally signals cells to stop proliferating. Mutated Ras inactivates negative feedback mechanism → cancer cells keep proliferating or producing (prevalent in pancreatic and colorectal cancers)
  • May counterbalance excessive proliferative signaling by forcing some cells into senescence → allows high growth and avoidance of cell death pressures
  • TKRs (Tyrosine Kinase Receptors): when stuck in "on" position → unregulated cellular proliferation → precursor for cellular transformation. More than 20 families of TKRs
  • PI3K and MAPK are the two targeted kinases from TKR stimulation
  • Signals can also originate from cancer cells and travel to normal cells → normal cells respond by supplying growth factors to malignant cells
📝 Targeted treatments: EGFR inhibitors, Cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors
🟠 B · Evading Growth Suppressors
  • Normal cells regulate growth with tumor suppressor genes → lead to senescence (cellular aging) and apoptosis (programmed cell death)
  • Cell growth also limited by contact inhibition — cell growth and division stop on physical contact with other cells
  • Some cancer cells have mutations that disable gatekeeper proteins controlling mitoses → inactivating cancer inhibition
  • pRB protein: (1) tumor suppressor for cell growth, (2) inhibition of DNA replication, (3) interaction with proteins to promote apoptosis, (4) promotion of cellular differentiation
  • p16 (CDKN2A/INK4a): cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A tumor suppressor gene that normally monitors cellular division and proliferation
  • TGF-β (transforming growth factor beta): transmembrane receptors signal intracellular gene family Smad to produce proteins for regulation of cellular growth and proliferation
  • E2F protein: transcription factor that affects gene expression; if mutated → altered gene expression → possibility of transformed cell
📝 Targeted treatment: Cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors
🟢 C · Resisting Cell Death (Apoptosis)
  • Normal cells experience apoptosis (programmed cell death). Process called autophagy allows normal cells to be broken down during cellular stress so organelles and cell contents can be reused
  • Necrotic cells release proinflammatory signals → cancer cells use these signals to recruit inflammatory cells and enhance tumor growth through microenvironment
  • Normal apoptosis triggered by TP53 — increases production of apoptotic proteins (NOXA) when significant DNA breaks or chromosomal abnormalities detected
  • Insufficient signaling (interleukins or insulin-like growth factor from epithelial cells) → prompts apoptosis
  • Hyperactive signaling by oncoproteins like Myc → another common trigger; interruption → prolonged cell life + accumulation of severe genetic abnormalities → tumorigenesis
  • BCL2 overexpression blocks apoptosis → follicular lymphoma
  • Potassium channels fill critical roles during apoptosis. Alterations of K⁺, Na⁺, Ca²⁺ ionic channels across cell membrane affect cell status and apoptosis
  • Loss of potassium → loss of cell volume → cell shrinkage → activates caspase cascade and other apoptotic changes
📝 Targeted treatment: Proapoptotic BH3 mimetics
🟣 D · Enabling Replicative Immortality
  • Normal cells have limited growth and division cycles because of senescence (nonproliferative but viable state) and crisis (involves cell death)
  • Cancer cells manipulate senescence and crisis mechanisms to survive
  • Normal cells have limited amounts of telomerase. Telomeres = protective DNA at ends of chromosomes that shorten with repeated cell duplication → when shortened ends reach critical level → signal cell death
  • Cancer cells contain high amounts of telomerase → adds protective telomeres → prevents telomere segment from shortening → allows continued cell replication → contributes to immortalization
  • Telomerase also protects cell from apoptosis → prevents death of malignant cells
  • Cancer stem cells known to have increased levels of telomerase → extended life span enhanced by protective telomeres at chromosome ends
  • Shorter telomeres → shorter life span. Cells with increased telomerase → longer telomeres → greater longevity
📝 Targeted treatment: Telomerase inhibitors
🔵 E · Inducing Angiogenesis
  • Angiogenesis = creation of new blood vessels from existing ones to provide nutrients and remove waste products
  • In normal tissues: during embryogenesis, wound healing, female reproductive cycling; otherwise dormant
  • Cancer cells secrete VEGF, bFGF, PDGF → stimulate angiogenesis to support continued tumor growth → supply nutrients
  • "Tumors cannot grow beyond 2 to 3 mm³ nor metastasize without new vasculature"
  • Tumor vasculature is less organized than normal → blood flow is chaotic → leading to hypoxia — considered the most important trigger of angiogenesis — and acidosis
  • Acidosis decreases therapeutic effectiveness and contributes to resistant clonal expansion
  • Hypoxia reinforced by abnormal vessels → stimulating production of more VEGF → endothelial cells instructed via D114/notch signaling to grow vessel sprouts → defective blood vessels → enhanced tumor hypoxia → vicious cycle
  • Hypoxic cancer cells induced by antiangiogenic inhibitors may invade nearby tissues in quest for access to vascularized tissue
📝 Targeted treatment: Inhibitors of VEGF signaling, PARP inhibitors
🔴 F · Activating Invasion & Metastasis
  • Normal cells have developmental regulatory program called EMT (Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition) — causes epithelial cells to lose cell polarity and cell-cell adhesion → become mesenchymal cells with invasive properties. Involved in mesoderm/neural tube formation, wound healing, and organ fibrosis
  • Cancer cells appear to use this mechanism during invasion and metastasis
  • Cadherins (glycoproteins) mediate cell-cell interactions → downregulated in cancer → loss of adhesion
  • Integrins: selectively binds to extracellular membrane by identifying three specific amino acids. Malignant cells no longer respond to controls of cadherin and integrin molecules when in contact with mitogens
  • Integrins activated by Src family kinases (SFK), sometimes in combination with RTK signaling molecules such as ErbB2 and Met
  • Mutated CDH1 (codes for E-cadherin) associated with gastric and lobular breast cancers
📝 Targeted treatment: Inhibitors of HGF/c-Met
🟡 G · Altered Energy Metabolism (Warburg Effect)
  • Cancer cells use aerobic glycolysis for energy production even in the presence of oxygen — also called the Warburg effect
  • Cancer cells "addicted" to using both glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration to provide energy (ATP) to the rapidly growing cancer cell
  • In tumors, 66% of glucose consumed is changed to lactic acid; in healthy cells there is no net production of lactic acid
  • GLUT1 transports glucose into cytoplasm or provides glucose converted to lactic acid
  • Driven by oncogenes: AKT, RAS, MYC — also associated with cell proliferation and avoidance of cell death
  • HIF-1α activates glycolysis and inhibits oxygen consumption. MYC stimulates aerobic glycolysis associated with HIF-1α
  • p53: acts as first line of defense against glucose uptake by preventing GLUT1 expression; also inhibits glycolysis and promotes mitochondrial respiration
  • IDH1 and IDH2 somatic mutations associated with OXPHOS mutations → ~80% of low-grade gliomas, ~30% of normal AML
ATP Production Comparison:
🔴 Oxygenated cancer cell: 1 glucose → 36 ATP + 10 glucose → 20 lactic acid → 20 ATP = 56 ATP total
🟠 Unoxygenated cancer cell: 13 glucose → 26 ATP
🟢 Normal oxygenated cell: 1 glucose → 36 ATP
📝 Targeted treatment: Aerobic glycolysis inhibitors
🔵 H · Evading Immune Destruction
  • Surveillance by immune system = real-time vigilante mechanism that monitors for emergent cancer cells and destroys most before they develop into a rapidly growing tumor
  • Deficiencies in T lymphocytes, T helper cells, and NK cells increase risk of cancer development
  • Cancer cells can evade T-cell response by expressing PD-L1 to block T-cell recognition → class of anticancer therapy called checkpoint inhibitors targets PD-L1
  • More than 80% of malignancies have nonviral causes; among individuals with deficiencies in CD4+ Th1 helper cells, CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, or NK cells → increased incidence of tumors
  • Lectins: glycoproteins that enable carbohydrate binding and immunomodulatory/antitumor effects. Alterations in lectin binding → leukocytes adhere to malignant cells → malignant cell escapes surveillance and travels to distant sites under guise of normal/abnormal cells
  • MDSCs (Myeloid Derived Suppressor Cells): exert immunosuppressive effects by paralyzing cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and decreasing NK cell activity
📝 Targeted treatment: Immune activating anti-CTLA4 mAb, anti-PD-L1
🟣 I · Evasion of Senescence
  • Senescence = stress response that blocks cell proliferation and prevents tumor progression in normal cells
  • Premalignant senescence must be overcome if cell is to evolve into malignant state
  • If capacity for senescence is impaired → can be "forced" into action by bypassing important oncogenic pathway or restoring damaged tumor suppressors
  • Hayflick and Moorhead: normal cells have finite number of cellular divisions before they rest "long term" from cycling. Senescence now believed to be due to: lack of reaction to growth factor stimulation, unsustainable metabolic activity, changes in cell morphology including diminished length of telomeres and reduced/inactivated telomerase activity
  • Activation of oncogenes or loss of tumor suppressor genes (PTEN, RB1, NF1, INPP4B) can also trigger senescence
  • Mutation of tumor suppressor genes can prevent senescence → allowing malignant activation
  • Noncoding RNAs (miR-20a) promote senescence induction through downregulation of LRF (ZBTB7A)
🔴 J · Genomic Instability
  • Instability of genome, inflammation, angiogenesis, and fibrosis enable tumor progression by fostering maintenance of gene mutations through defective DNA repair
  • Vogelstein was first to note "all cancer is genetic" — based on central dogma: DNA → RNA → protein, even in malignancy
  • Driver mutations allow thousands of mutations to occur. Normal repair mechanisms overlook DNA damage → incorrect DNA sequences → increased probabilities of mutations and growth advantage
  • HNPCC: characterized by Microsatellite Instability (MSI) → Normal cells have consistent length of nucleotides; cancer cells show variable length
📝 Targeted treatment: PARP inhibitors
🟠 K · Tumor-Promoting Inflammation
  • By turn of 21st century, researchers realized tumor-associated inflammatory response fosters tumorigenesis and progression
  • Innate immune response has influence at intersection of inflammation and cancer progression
  • Inflammation supplies bioactive molecules to tumor microenvironment: growth factors sustaining proliferative signaling; survival factors limiting cell death; pro-angiogenic factors; ECM-modifying enzymes facilitating angiogenesis, invasion, metastasis; inductive signals leading to EMT
  • Inflammatory cells release ROS (reactive oxygen species) — oxygen ions, superoxides, and peroxide → mutagenic to cells in close proximity → accelerate malignant transformation
  • Much research links inflammation associated with chronic disease to earliest stages of neoplastic progression
📝 Targeted treatment: Selective anti-inflammatory drugs
🔵 L · Disruption of Epithelial Adhesion & Polarity
  • Normal epithelial cells held in place through tight junctions and adhesion molecules → ions unable to pass through space between cells
  • Transmembrane proteins (integrin and cadherin) support tight junctions
  • Normal cells will not respond to mitogens as long as restrained by cadherin-associated contact inhibition
  • Malignant cells no longer respond to controls of cadherin and integrin molecules when in contact with mitogens
  • SFKs activate E-cadherin → tagged for degradation
  • Altered surface charge density: malignant cell membrane gains more phospholipids (especially anions) → more negative charge → inhibit apoptosis, contribute to longevity, increase loss of contact inhibition → enhance ability to spread, invade, and metastasize
📝 Targeted treatment: Inhibitors of HGF/c-Met
🟢 M · Matrix Degradation & Gain in Motility
  • Malignant cell membrane gains proteins, glycoproteins, and glycolipids with altered mobility on surface membrane due to ectopic hormone production and/or malignant changes specific to tissue type
  • Outcomes: avoid immune surveillance, promote invasion, and metastasize
  • Cells have a skeleton with interior and exterior functions: shape/mechanical strength, growth/division/signaling, mobility
  • Cytoskeleton: permits substances to move within cell. On exterior membrane, microtubules evoke rigidity. Internally, they promote movement of organelles within cytoplasm
  • When malignancy manifests → cell loses external cytoskeleton control → loses rigidity → rounds up → more amenable to continued cellular division
  • Multiple protein types participate in changes associated with malignant transformation and locomotion

🔬 08 · Tumor Microenvironment ⭐⭐
The stroma consists of connective tissue, blood vessels, immune inflammatory cells such as macrophages and lymphocytes, and associated fibroblasts. Communication and signaling between tumor cells and normal stroma cells lead to tumor growth and metastasis through multitude of reciprocal pathways.
TAMs (Tumor-Associated Macrophages)
  • Both anti-tumorigenic and pro-tumorigenic macrophages
  • Influence: immune evasion, hypoxia, inflammation, angiogenic switch, macrophage polarization switch (reversible?)
  • Release MMPs, cathepsins, type II cytokines
  • Inhibition of cytolytic granule release
  • M1-phenotype blocked
  • Tumor-associated Ag presentation inhibited
CAFs (Cancer-Associated Fibroblasts)
  • Second class of connective tissue induced by cancer cells
  • Switch to unusual aerobic form of glycolysis
  • Secrete lactate (along with tumor cells) plus pyruvate to fuel cancer cell proliferation
  • Influence via TGF-β, MCP1, PDGF, FGF proteases
  • Promote EMT
MDSCs
  • Myeloid derived suppressor cells
  • Express macrophage marker CD11b and neutrophil marker Gr1
  • Exert immunosuppressive effects by paralyzing CTLs and decreasing NK cell activity
  • These tumor-promoting cells are designated MDSCs
Signaling Molecules
  • EGF, VEGF, VEGF-C, VEGF-D
  • TGF-β, CXCL5, CXCR2
  • MMPs, integrins, ECM proteins (matrisome)
  • CSF-1 → drives immune suppression and angiogenesis
EGFR / TGF-α in Colon Metastasis
  • TGF-α or EGFR signaling pathway plays role in some colon cancer metastases
  • Some metastatic colon cancer cells produce 5× more EGFR compared with nonmetastatic cells
  • TGF-α initiates angiogenic processes in normal endothelial cells
  • When combined with VEGF production → lymphangiogenesis → tumor cells may spread to regional lymph nodes
  • Cetuximab (monoclonal antibody): blocks EGFR ligand binding; used in EGFR-positive patients to reduce primary tumor size and lymphatic spread
T cells, NK cells, Dendritic cells
  • Can provide antitumor immunity when functional
  • Inhibition of B and T cell responses occurs when tumor suppresses them
  • T reg cells and NK cells present in microenvironment
  • Lymphatic vessels, blood vessels form tumor vasculature

📍 09 · Metastasis ⭐⭐⭐
90%of cancer-related deaths related to metastatic disease
30%of newly diagnosed solid tumors present with metastatic disease
Metastasis = the spread of cancer cells from the site of the original tumor or organ to distant tissues and organs in the body. The presence of metastatic disease at diagnosis is an important prognostic factor because it indicates advanced disease.
Metastatic Cascade — Step by Step
1
Invasion of ECM: Downregulate cadherin glycoproteins mediating cell-cell interactions. Produce/stimulate stromal cells to secrete proteases that degrade ECM. Attach to ECM proteins for mobility. Through locomotion (complex signaling of proteases, cytokines, motility factors) → tumor cells migrate through ECM and gain access to vascular basement membrane.
2
Intravasation: Tumor cell emboli enter blood or lymphatic vessels.
3
Survival in Circulation: Tumor cells susceptible to destruction by mechanical stress, immune defenses, and apoptosis due to lack of cell-cell adhesion. To survive: tend to travel in clumps, may combine with platelets to form platelet-tumor aggregates, and may interact with coagulation factors to create emboli. Research uses circulating tumor cells as "liquid biopsy" for cancer screening, estimation of metastatic relapse risk, monitoring therapeutic response.
4
Extravasation: Exit blood vessels at distant site. Adhere to basement membrane at new location.
5
Metastatic Growth: Form metastatic deposit → trigger angiogenesis → grow. Cancer cells may have a "homing device" — cytokines induce host cells to produce substances that recognize receptors on cancer cells → may promote growth in these distant tissues.
Pathways of Cancer Dissemination
Direct & Lymphatic
  • Direct invasion to an adjoining organ
  • Seeding throughout a body cavity (e.g., ovarian cancer → peritoneal cavity)
  • Lymphatic: entrapment at first lymph node = "sentinel lymph node"
  • "Skip metastasis": cells bypass the first node and reach more distant sites
Blood Vessel Dissemination
  • Arterial spread: through pulmonary capillary beds or pulmonary arteriovenous (AV) shunts. Arteries have thicker walls → less readily penetrated than veins
  • Venous spread: tumor cells often go to the first capillary bed encountered. Liver and lung are the most frequent sites of metastasis
Predilection for Specific Sites — Influenced By:
  • Patterns of blood flow
  • Cell receptors and genes that direct the cell to travel to specific sites
  • Tumor cell production of adhesion molecules that prefer certain distant organs
  • Chemical signals and growth factors found only in selected organs
  • Inhibitor substances produced by organs not typically sites for metastatic growth
  • "Cross-talk" between cancer cells and normal host cells — cancer cells may release cytokines that induce host cells to produce substances that recognize receptors on cancer cells ("homing device")

⚡ 10 · Key Signaling Pathways ⭐⭐
PI3K / AKT / mTOR Pathway
  • PI3K activates phosphorylation to nudge AKT and localize it on cell membrane
  • When cell glucose is elevated → cell more likely to proliferate; less glucose → quiescence via p21 tumor suppressor protein
  • AKT signal → activation of mTOR → promotes expression of protein kinase that regulates cell growth, proliferation, motility, survival, protein synthesis, transcription
  • Associated with malignancy → hyperactive → enabling cellular proliferation and decreasing apoptosis
  • PTEN antagonizes PI3K and negatively regulates MAPK → constraints on cell propagation
  • Mutations in genes or proteins expressed by genes associated with this pathway → been target of multiple therapies
RAS / MAPK Pathway
  • KRAS gene produces K-Ras protein → regulates cell division through intracellular RAS/MAPK signaling
  • Protein travels from cytoplasm to nucleus → participates in specialized functions: proliferation, differentiation, senescence
  • BRAF gene transmits extracellular signals to cell's nucleus → essential for normal prenatal development
  • When mutated → affects proliferation, differentiation, migration, and apoptosis
NF-κB Pathway
  • When p53 is mutated → promotes TNFα signaling → activation of macrophages and regulation of immune cells
  • This mutation affects two pathways downstream: NF-κB (alter control of DNA transcription) and JNK (diminish apoptosis)
  • Selectins and chemokines (including NF-κB) and their receptors encourage invasion, migration, and metastasis (inflammation theory)
TKR Signaling
  • More than 20 families of TKRs
  • When phosphorylated to active state → affect variety of tissue needs
  • If TKR signals become stuck in "on" position → unregulated cellular proliferation → precursor for cellular transformation
  • Two targeted kinases: PI3K and MAPK
  • Signals can originate from cancer cells → travel to normal cells → normal cells respond by supplying growth factors to malignant cells
Four Signaling Circuits
Motility Circuits
  • APC (activated protein C) receptor influences innate immune response by decreasing natural killer and Th17 T-helper cells
  • Extracellular-intracellular matrix → stimulation of intracellular integrins → breakdown of matrix of cellular infrastructure
  • Nuclear transcription factor 4 (TCF4) stimulates tumor suppressor gene → indirect signaling to cMyc → changes in gene expression
Cytostasis & Differentiation Circuits
  • TGF-β transmembrane receptors signal intracellular Smad gene family to produce proteins for regulation (or dysregulation) of cellular growth and proliferation
  • p16 (CDKN2A/INK4a): normally monitors cellular division and proliferation
  • pRB (retinoblastoma gene protein): multiple actions as tumor suppressor
  • E2F protein: transcription factor that affects gene expression
Proliferation Circuits
  • Receptor tyrosine kinases promote proliferation through multiple signaling pathways
  • RTKs can activate Ras family of oncogenes and ultimately Myc oncogenes
  • Myc gene participates in cell-cycle progression as part of proliferation circuitry
  • Mutations prevent apoptosis and promote cellular transformation
  • Overexpression of mutated Myc gene frequently associated with hematopoietic tumors
Viability Circuits
  • Mediate the death and survival factors associated with cell mechanisms
  • Survival factors have receptors on cell membrane. Once activated through phosphorylation → limit cell death, facilitate angiogenesis, resist apoptosis, deplete immune response, produce ECM-modifying enzymes to promote invasion and metastasis
  • BCL2 gene product: mitochondrial membrane protein that blocks apoptosis
  • Death factors activated by transmembrane receptors (HIF-α and others) that signal core metabolic pathways driven by oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes → expression of inflammation-related pathways

✅ 11 · Key Takeaways ⭐⭐⭐
🧠 Metastasis contributes to the pain and suffering caused by cancer and is the major cause of death from cancer (Rote & Virshup, 2017).
🧠 Understanding the process of carcinogenesis and metastasis helps lead to therapies targeting the molecular changes associated with the disease.
🧠 Although there is growing understanding of genomics and factors contributing to cancer development, cancer cells remain adaptable. This flexibility creates challenges for the development of a single cure-all therapy.
🧠 TP53 is mutated in approximately 50% of all human cancers — the most common genetic target. It is the "guardian of the genome."
🧠 The Philadelphia chromosome (BCR-ABL translocation) in CML → tyrosine kinase → imatinib was the original targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor designed specifically to inhibit this pathway.
🧠 Epigenetic changes can be reversed — decitabine (hypomethylation) and HDAC inhibitors are examples of epigenetic therapies already in clinical use.
🧠 Checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-L1, anti-CTLA4 mAb) restore immune recognition of cancer cells by targeting the immune evasion hallmark.
🧠 Warburg effect: oxygenated cancer cell generates 56 ATP vs. 36 in normal cells. Cancer cells prefer aerobic glycolysis even with oxygen present.
🧠 Tumors cannot grow beyond 2–3 mm³ nor metastasize without new vasculature — making VEGF inhibitors clinically significant.
🧠 30% of newly diagnosed solid tumors present with metastatic disease; 90% of cancer-related deaths are related to metastasis.
🧠 No two cancers are alike — each tumor has a unique set of mutated genes ("snowflake" concept). Clonal evolution describes cells within a tumor accumulating genetic changes over time.
🧠 CSCs in G₀ (resting) phase are most resistant to treatment — can re-enter active phases later → explains cancer recurrence after apparent remission.
🧠 853 known germline variants exist that contribute to cancer development — BRCA1, BRCA2 are examples. When one is inherited → becomes more susceptible to certain types of cancer.
