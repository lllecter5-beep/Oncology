01
Lymphedema
Definition
It is an obstruction of lymphatic system which is lead to accumulation of lymph fluids in interstitial spaces.
Primary
  • Genetic or familial abnormalities present at birth
Secondary
  • Damage or destruction of the lymphatic system through life
Incidents
  • Increase the chance with lymph resection
  • Could be acute for days or permanent
Risk Factors
Noncancer Related Cancer Diagnosis Related Treatment Related
  • Poor nutrition
  • Genetics
  • Thrombophlebitis
  • Skin inflammation or chronic disorders of the skin
  • Obesity / elevated body mass index (BMI)
  • Tumor invasion and/or advanced disease
  • Infection — affected extremity, concurrent illness
  • Air travel with suboptimal cabin pressure, long distance travel, prolonged immobilization
  • Traumatic injury to affected extremity
  • Excessive physical use of affected extremity; prolonged standing (lower extremity)
  • Surgical lymph node dissection, number of lymph nodes removed, type of surgery, seroma formation after surgery, or delayed wound healing
  • Radiation therapy — location and formation of scars or fibrosis
Physical Examination
  • Tightness of clothing, shoes, wristwatch, jewelry
  • Visible puffiness
  • Pain, stiffness, weakness, numbness, paresthesia of affected extremity
  • Redness, warmth of affected extremity
  • Tends to occur distal to proximal
  • Thickening, pitting, and erythema of skin; peau d'orange
  • Increased pigmentation / superficial veins, stasis dermatitis
  • Induration with nonpitting edema
  • Secondary cellulitis
  • Decreased range of motion, feeling of heaviness of affected extremity
Assessment Tools
A. Screening Tools
  • Circumference measurements
    • Arm measured 5 and 10 cm above and below the olecranon process (Elbow) and compared with the other extremity
    • Leg measured at the level of the calf
  • Water displacement method to measure limb volume
    • Limitations due to difficulty using in clinic setting and does not indicate lymphedema location
  • Perometer — using infrared light to measure fluid volume of the limb
  • Bioelectrical impedance — uses electrical currents through the regions to determine volume
B. Imaging & Laboratory Tests
  • Lymphoscintigraphy — radioactive mapping of lymphatic vessels
  • Ultrasound — for evaluation of tissue and fluid
  • CT / MRI / PET scan — not approved for evaluation of lymphedema but can be used to evaluate soft tissue or a possible mass
Clinical Staging / Grading

Staging of lymphedema by examination evaluates the progression of the disease. Grading evaluates severity of signs and symptoms.

A. Staging of Lymphedema (Clinical Stages)
StageDescription
Stage 1Mild and spontaneously reversible.
• Slight heaviness of the extremity.
• Skin is smooth textured with pitting edema.
• May experience pain and erythema.
Stage 2Moderate and irreversible.
• May develop tissue fibrosis.
• Skin appears stretched, shiny, and has non-pitting edema.
Stage 3Severe, irreversible lymphostatic elephantiasis.
• Skin is discolored, stretched, and firm.
• Rare in breast cancer patients.
B. Grading of Lymphedema (Severity by Size/Function)
GradeDescription
Grade 1• Swelling with pitting edema.
• 5%–10% difference in size or mass of limbs.
Grade 2• Obvious obstruction.
• Taut skin.
• 10%–30% difference in size or mass.
Grade 3• Limb appears disfigured.
• Interferes with Activities of Daily Living (ADLs).
• More than 30% difference in size or mass.
Grade 4• Often associated with malignancy.
• Disabling condition; may require removal of affected limb.
• 5%–10% size/mass difference (similar to Grade 1).
Management
  • Treat suspected infections with early antibiotic treatment
  • Manage acute and chronic pain
  • Use interventions in conjunction with other modalities to reduce symptoms associated with lymphedema
★A. Recommended for Practice — 1. Complete Decongestive Therapy (CDT) — Standard of Care
⭐ Exam Favorite — Lymphedema
  • CDT = Gold Standard treatment for lymphedema
  • MCQ: "What is the standard treatment for lymphedema?" → Complete Decongestive Therapy
  • 4 components: MLD + Compression + Exercise + Skin Care
Phase I — Intensive Phase (2–6 weeks / 5 days a week)
ComponentDescription
1. Manual Lymphatic Drainage (MLD)• Gentle, hands-on massage technique.
• Stimulates lymph flow and re-routes fluid around blocked areas.
2. Compression Therapy• Short-stretch bandaging applied after MLD.
• Reduces limb volume and prevents fluid reaccumulation.
3. Skin Care• Prevents infections (e.g., cellulitis).
• Use of pH-balanced cleansers and moisturizers.
4. Exercise• Light, controlled movement while wearing compression.
• Enhances lymphatic and venous return.
Phase II — Maintenance Phase (start immediately after Phase I — count for life)
ComponentDescription
Compression Garments• Custom-fitted elastic garments (e.g., sleeves, stockings).
• Worn daily to maintain volume reduction.
Self-MLD (if trained)• Patients perform simplified MLD techniques on themselves.
Skin Monitoring & Hygiene• Ongoing care to detect early infection or skin breakdown.
Lifestyle Modifications• Avoid heat exposure, limb trauma, tight clothing.
• Elevate limb when possible.
• Maintain healthy weight.
2. Likely to Be Effective for Practice in Lymphedema
  • Axillary reverse mapping (ARM) — Surgical technique to identify and spare lymphatic drainage of the arm during axillary lymph node dissection, reducing risk of lymphedema.
  • Exercise — Progressive, supervised exercise programs (aerobic + resistance training) help maintain lymph flow, prevent stiffness, and reduce lymphedema risk.
  • Prevention and early intervention protocols
    • a) Early evaluation
      • Regular screening and baseline limb measurements (e.g., perometer, bioimpedance)
      • Detecting subclinical lymphedema early improves outcomes
    • b) Referrals to lymphedema specialist, advocacy groups, National Lymphedema Network
  • 3. Weight management — Obesity is a known risk factor for chronic lymphedema.
B. Other Considerations
  • Avoid prolonged standing.
  • Elevate affected extremity.
  • Avoid extreme heat — may worsen the swelling (e.g., hot tubs).
  • Skin care program with proper bathing, drying, lubrication.
  • Compression garments when flying.
Patient Education
  • Signs and symptoms to report (e.g., high risk for infection).
  • Need for lifelong follow-up.
  • A low-sodium, high-fiber, weight-control diet.
  • Importance of skin care to maintain skin integrity and prevent infections.
  • Education to eliminate safety hazards to prevent injury.
  • Maintain healthy weight.
  • Limit time in extreme temperatures.
  • Wear loose-fitting clothes.
02
Edema
Definition
Fluid accumulation in interstitial spaces. It is the movement of fluid from the vascular space into the interstitial space by an alteration in one or more of the following:
Mechanism Explanation Examples
↑ Capillary pressure Raised hydrostatic pressure — when volume of blood is expanded or with obstruction; extrinsic sodium and water retention by the kidneys caused by renal failure, reduction of cardiac output or systemic vascular resistance can cause edema. Stimulation of the renin–angiotensin system causes an increase in pressure in the vascular bed (forcing fluid into interstitial spaces) Heart disease, renal disease, cirrhosis, deep venous thrombosis (DVT)
↑ Capillary permeability From vascular injury Burns, radiation, drug reactions, infection; treatment with interleukin-2 or vascular endothelial growth factors
Obstruction of lymph system Lymphedema —
↓ Plasma oncotic pressure Results in increased fluid in the tissues; albumin causes retention of fluid in the vascular bed. When albumin is decreased, fluid leaks into interstitial spaces Proteinuria, hepatic failure, malabsorption, protein malnutrition
Raised hydrostatic pressure Fluid driven from the capillaries into the interstitial spaces Venous obstruction, fluid retention, prolonged standing
May Be Associated With the Following
  • Lymphatic obstruction by tumor or DVT.
  • Cancer — common with kidney, liver, ovarian.
  • Systemic conditions — heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, liver failure, thyroid disease, obstructive sleep apnea.
  • Medications may include hormones, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), calcium channel blockers, tricyclic antidepressants, steroids, interleukin-2 (IL-2) therapy, corticosteroids, beta blockers.
  • Chemotherapy (i.e., cisplatin, docetaxel, gemcitabine)
  • Monoclonal antibodies (i.e., bevacizumab)
  • Targeted therapies (i.e., imatinib mesylate)
  • Antiangiogenetic agents (i.e., thalidomide)
  • Allergic response or septic shock, which leads to histamine release
  • Poor nutrition, hypoproteinemia, hypoalbuminemia
  • Iatrogenic causes — plasma expanders, intravenous (IV) fluid overload, blood component therapy
  • Burns, trauma, sepsis
  • Allergic reactions
  • Malignant ascites
Risk Factors
  • Preexisting cardiac, renal, liver disease, thyroid disease.
  • Cancer (i.e., kidney, liver or ovarian cancers).
  • Cancer treatments (i.e., cisplatin, docetaxel).
  • Decreased mobility.
  • Long-distance travel.
  • Prior history of edema.
  • Certain medications.
  • DVT.
  • Hypertension.
Management
  • Treat the underlying cause (i.e., congestive heart failure [CHF], nephrotic syndrome, liver failure/cirrhosis, thrombophlebitis, lymphedema, DVT).
    • Diuretics.
    • Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or, if not tolerated, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs).
    • Beta blockers.
    • Analgesics as needed.
  • Elevate extremities above level of heart.
  • Compression stockings.
  • Maintain lubrication of skin.
  • Bed rest to promote diuresis; reposition every 2 hours.
  • Fluid restriction.
  • Treatment of underlying cause.
  • Monitor intake and output, electrolytes, serum albumin.
  • Protect extremity from injury.
  • Walk or other exercise.
  • Protect from extreme temperatures.
  • Provide patient education.
    • Low-sodium, well-balanced diet, protein in diet for those with hypoalbuminemia.
    • Employ appropriate skin care strategies.
    • Avoid prolonged standing or sitting with legs crossed.
    • Avoid hepatotoxic drugs and alcohol.
    • Fluid restriction may be warranted.
    • Walk or exercise to maintain muscle strength and joint range of motion.
    • Perform daily weights and report greater than a 3- to 4-pound gain.
    • Educate patient and family regarding risks for electrolyte disturbance and possible interventions if symptoms occur.
    • Wear support hose with instruction on how to apply them appropriate.

03
Malignant Pericardial Effusion
Definition
Accumulation of fluid in the pericardial sac that affects cardiac function, resulting in decreased cardiac output.
Metastatic Disease → Cardiac Space
  • Through local advancement, spread by blood or lymph systems or obstruction from adenopathy
  • Examples: Most common 1st lung or 2nd breast cancer; can occur in lymphoma
Treatment Complications
  • From radiation fibrosis causing damage to structure
  • Chemotherapy causing damage to endothelial cells or cardiomyocytes, or both
Infection
  • Infection in an immunocompromised patient
Incidence
  • Once diagnosed, prognosis is poor.
  • Varies by malignant process involved.
  • Pericardial effusions are present in approximately 1% to 20% of patients with cancer.
  • Approximately 50% of pericardial effusions in patients with cancer are malignant.
  • Patients generally surviving less than 6 months after its occurrence regardless of treatment.
Physiology (from Yarbro Ch.33)
The heart is surrounded by the pericardium — a thin, fibrous sac composed of two membranous layers: the visceral pericardium (inner membrane connected to the heart surface) and the parietal pericardium (outer fibrous membrane in direct contact with the chest wall). The pericardial sac between the two membranes normally contains 15 to 50 mL of clear, serous, lubricating fluid that decreases friction during cardiac contraction.
Slowly Accumulating Effusion
  • Pericardium can stretch and accommodate as much as 4 L of fluid without symptoms
  • Gradual onset — body adapts progressively
  • Can accumulate large volumes with minimal symptoms
Rapidly Accumulating Effusion
  • Normal compensatory mechanisms unable to adapt with rapid fluid development of only 100 to 200 mL
  • Results in severe cardiac compromise and symptoms
  • Can lead to cardiac tamponade

Malignant pericardial effusions occur as a result of excess fluid accumulation from obstruction of the venous and lymphatic drainage by direct tumor invasion or from lymphatic or hematogenous spread. The majority of malignant pericardial effusions result from blockage of the lymphatic drainage of the heart. The effects of pericardial effusion accumulation depend on the rate of fluid accumulation, the physical compliance capacity of the pericardial cavity, ventricular function, myocardial size, and blood volume.

Clinical Manifestations (from Yarbro Ch.33)

Symptoms associated with pericardial effusion are related to the rate of effusion accumulation.

Common Symptoms
  • Cough, fever, dyspnea, orthopnea
  • Chest pain
  • Edema
  • Weakness, dysphagia, syncope, palpitations
Most Common Signs
  • Rales, tachypnea, tachycardia
  • Hypotension
  • Peripheral edema
  • Paradoxical pulse (pulsus paradoxus)
  • Distant heart sounds
  • Pericardial rub
⚠In most patients with cancer, malignant pericardial effusions may be asymptomatic and go undiagnosed until the late stages of the disease. The clinician should have a high index of suspicion for pericardial involvement whenever patients with cancer present with cardiovascular symptoms.
Risk Factors
1. Pre-existing or Coexisting Conditions
  • Cardiac disease
  • Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)
  • Bacterial endocarditis
2. Radiation Therapy (RT)
  • ≥ 3000 cGy to more than 33% of the heart
  • Fraction size > 300 cGy/day → ↑ risk of late cardiac toxicity
3. Chemotherapy & Biotherapy Agents
  • Chemotherapy:
    • Cytosine arabinoside
    • Cyclophosphamide
    • Busulfan
    • Doxorubicin (anthracycline) → well-known cardiotoxic
    • Gemcitabine
  • Biological Agents:
    • Interferon
    • Interleukin-2 (IL-2)
    • Interleukin-11 (IL-11)
    • Granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF)
  • Targeted Agents:
    • Imatinib
    • Dasatinib
  • Other Drugs:
    • Arsenic trioxide
    • All-trans retinoic acid (ATRA)
4. Rare Causes
  • Hemorrhagic tamponade from:
    • Direct cardiac injury
    • Infectious causes
5. Malignancies (Commonest Causes of Cardiac Metastases)
  • Lung cancer → most common
  • Breast cancer → second most common
  • Melanoma → highest propensity
  • Lymphoma
  • Leukemia
6. Thoracic Lymphatic Obstruction

Secondary to:

  • Malignant lymphadenopathy
  • HIV-associated lymphadenopathy
  • Hematologic malignancies
  • Lymphangioleiomyomatosis
  • Extramedullary multiple myeloma
  • Thymic cancer

Assessment — Physical Exam (from Yarbro Ch.33)

The physical assessment should include a thorough examination focusing on the cardiac system, taking note of the patient's color, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, breath sounds, jugular venous distension, peripheral pulses, heart sounds, and abdominal girth.

Diagnostic Studies (from Ch.33)
  • Echocardiogram — most commonly used diagnostic tool; reliable, noninvasive test that can evaluate the effect of a pericardial effusion on ventricular function. Gold standard = two-dimensional echocardiography, which can identify location and amount of effusion and presence of pericardial or intracardiac masses
  • ECG changes — tachycardia, atrial and ventricular arrhythmia, low QRS voltage, ST-segment elevations, T-wave inversion
  • Chest radiograph — cardiomegaly, bilateral pleural effusions, mediastinal widening, hilar lymphadenopathy; heart may have "water-bottle" appearance (indicative of a large pericardial effusion)
  • CT scan — can identify pericardial fluid in amounts as small as 50 mL, as well as pericardial thickening and masses; more time-consuming than echocardiography
  • Pericardiocentesis (diagnostic) — invasive procedure; reserved for symptomatic individuals from a large effusion. Patient at 30–45° angle; local anesthetic administered; 18- or 19-gauge needle passed cephalad at ~45°. Fluid analyzed to determine if transudate or exudate; sent for bacterial, fungal, mycobacterial cultures and cytology. Positive cytologic examination confirmed in only 50% to 60% of pericardial effusions in patients with cancer. Malignant pericardial fluid is exudative or bloody in appearance. Complications: myocardial puncture, coronary artery or vein laceration, hemopericardium, laceration of internal mammary artery, pneumothorax, liver and aortic injury
Therapeutic Approaches — Detailed (from Yarbro Ch.33)
🎯Goals: relief of symptoms, identification of positive cytology, and prevention of fluid reaccumulation. Patient's diagnosis, stage of disease, and performance status should be considered in selecting the treatment approach.
🚨 Cardiac Tamponade — Oncologic Emergency

Compression of the heart from pericardial fluid accumulation → impairs cardiac filling → ↓ cardiac output

Beck's Triad
  • Hypotension
  • JVD (Jugular Venous Distension)
  • Muffled heart sounds
Additional Signs
  • Pulsus paradoxus > 13 mmHg
  • Tachycardia
  • Cool, clammy extremities
  • Narrow pulse pressure
Emergency Treatment
  • Pericardiocentesis — immediate percutaneous drainage
  • Oxygen therapy + hemodynamic monitoring
  • IV fluids to maintain preload
  • Avoid diuretics and vasodilators (worsen hypotension)
Approach Details
Pericardiocentesis
1st line		 Most frequently performed in symptomatic patients as a therapeutic procedure. High success rate but fluid reaccumulation occurs in approximately 50% of patients. Other definitive medical or surgical treatment is necessary to prevent recurrence. Nursing care: explanation of procedure; semi-Fowler's position; maintaining asepsis; having available defibrillator, oxygen, and emergency medications; continuous monitoring of patient and ECG during and after procedure to detect any cardiac or pulmonary abnormalities.
Subxiphoid Pericardiostomy
(Pericardial Window)
Most commonly undertaken — preferred surgical treatment		 Performed under local anesthesia. Small, vertical skin incision in subxiphoid region → anterior pericardium incised → fluid drained → sample obtained for pathologic studies → pericardial tube placed in upper abdominal wall into pericardial space. Overall morbidity and mortality rate is low; recurrence rate ~3.5%. Nursing care same as for pericardiocentesis.
Balloon Pericardiotomy Nonsurgical procedure to create a pericardial window. Guide wire threaded percutaneously into pericardium → balloon-dilating catheter inserted and inflated to create pericardial window for fluid drainage. Helpful in management of massive malignant pericardial effusions in patients with poor performance status.
Pericardiocentesis with Sclerosing Agent Instillation Sclerosing agents instilled to obliterate pericardial space by inducing an inflammatory response → fibrosis and sclerosis of the pericardium. Agents used: Tetracycline Doxycycline Bleomycin Cisplatin. Tetracycline and doxycycline have been most extensively evaluated. Controls pericardial effusion for longer than 30 days in more than 90% of patients.
Surgical Approaches Transthoracic pericardial window via thoracotomy and pericardiectomy — generally reserved for individuals who require repeated pericardiocentesis and are medically appropriate (general anesthesia and thoracotomy required). Risks: pneumonia, pleural effusion, cardiac arrhythmia, respiratory failure, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism. Preferred option: video-assisted thoracoscopy (less invasive than thoracotomy). Pleurectomy — stripping of parietal pleura — is 100% effective in controlling malignant pleural effusions and is primary therapeutic modality for some types of mesothelioma.
Radiation Therapy Generally indicated for patients with leukemia/lymphoma or breast carcinoma. Requires several weeks of daily treatments or prolonged hospitalization. Usual dose: 100 to 200 cGy daily for approximately 3 to 4 weeks. Can potentially cause acute pericarditis or myocarditis.
Chemotherapy If tumor responsive (lymphoma, leukemia, breast cancer). May be combined with hormonal therapy for appropriate tumor types.

Cardiovascular Toxicity Related to Cancer Therapy
Definition
Damage to the cardiac muscle, conduction system, coronary arteries, valves, and/or pericardium related to cancer therapies that may cause alterations in cardiac function.
⚠Important (from Ch.33): HER2-targeted therapy (e.g., Trastuzumab) combined with anthracyclines = highest risk of cardiotoxicity. Trastuzumab alone causes reversible LV dysfunction, but when combined with anthracyclines the risk of HF increases significantly. These agents should not be given concurrently.

Incidence
A. Acute Reactions
  • Infrequent; reversible
  • Occur within 24 hours of drug administration
  • Usually self-limiting and cease when the drug is stopped
  • May not require discontinuation of the drug
B. Early Onset (<1 year)
  • Infrequent
C. Late Onset (>1 year after therapy)
  • Occurs with cumulative doses of drugs
  • Dilated cardiomyopathy can occur
Risk Factors
Patient Factors
  • Preexisting heart disease, HTN, hyperlipidemia
  • History of smoking
  • Age younger than 15 years or advanced age > 65
  • Certain cardiotoxic drugs
  • Exceeding recommended total doses of chemotherapy or high dose in short period
  • Radiation treatment field which includes the heart
  • Acute cardiac event during treatment
  • Combination chest radiation with anthracycline
  • Others: female, obesity, comorbid disease, and previous cancer treatment that had cardiotoxicity
Doses That Place Patient at Risk
  • Doxorubicin > 550 mg/m²
  • Liposomal doxorubicin > 900 mg/m²
  • Epirubicin > 720 mg/m²
  • Mitoxantrone > 120 mg/m²
  • Idarubicin > 90 mg/m²
Physical Examination
  • A. Orthostatic symptoms, arrhythmias, jugular vein distention, bilateral pedal edema, presence of S₃ or S₄, murmurs with valve abnormalities, and decreased cardiac EF. Tachycardia — early sign in anthracycline toxicity.
  • B. Shortness of breath, dyspnea, orthopnea, and nonproductive cough
  • C. Exercise intolerance, fatigue
  • D. Weight gain
  • E. Syncope
Imaging & Laboratory Tests
A. Electrocardiogram (ECG) Changes
  • Premature atrial contractions (PACs) and premature ventricular contractions (PVCs)
  • Nonspecific ST-T wave changes
B. Echocardiogram or Multigated Acquisition (MUGA) Scan
  • Left ventricular function monitoring — usually assessing ejection fraction (EF)
    • A decrease in EF to <45% or a drop >5% from baseline should prompt consideration to:
      • Hold or discontinue the cardiotoxic drug
      • Continue monitoring EF until improvement
      • Resumption of the drug should be individualized
  • Detection of pericardial effusion
  • Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH)
C. Cardiac MRI
  • Considered the gold standard for evaluating:
    • Left ventricular volume
    • Mass
    • Function
  • However, it may be cost-prohibitive and not always accessible
D. BMI and Waist Circumference
  • Used to assess cardiovascular risk factors associated with obesity and metabolic syndrome
E. Laboratory Tests Impacting Cardiac Function
  • Important parameters include:
    • Electrolytes: Potassium, Magnesium, Calcium
    • Renal function
    • Thyroid function
    • Lipid profile
  • Electrolyte monitoring is critical, especially in patients receiving drugs that may prolong the QT interval
Management of Cardiotoxicity
I. Pharmacologic Management
  • Treatment is tailored to the underlying cardiac event (e.g., heart failure, arrhythmia, hypertension).
  • Use of lipid-lowering agents (e.g., statins) if hyperlipidemia is present.
  • Beta-blockers and ACE inhibitors:
    • Used to manage left ventricular dysfunction (LVD).
  • Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs):
    • When used concurrently with anthracyclines, may prevent cardiac damage.
  • ACE inhibitors and calcium channel blockers:
    • For the treatment of hypertension (HTN).
  • Minimize the risk of QT interval prolongation:
    • Avoid drug–drug interactions that affect QT.
    • Correct and monitor electrolyte imbalances, especially potassium, magnesium, and calcium.
  • Cardioprotective strategies for anthracycline use:
    • Dexrazoxane (Zinecard) — administer in metastatic breast cancer patients who require >300 mg/m² of doxorubicin. It is an intracellular chelating agent that interferes with iron-mediated free radical generation thought to be responsible for anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathy. Note: not recommended for use in adjuvant treatment of breast cancer or at initiation of anthracycline therapy.
    • Liposomal doxorubicin — use to reduce cardiotoxicity risk; encapsulation reduces peak plasma levels and myocardial exposure
  • Anticoagulation for patients at risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE).
  • Valve surgery may be required in patients with radiation-induced valvular disease.
  • Comprehensive disease management:
    • Includes both oncologic and cardiac conditions simultaneously.
  • Radiation therapy (RT) techniques:
    • Use cardiac-sparing approaches to minimize cardiac exposure.
II. Nonpharmacologic Management
  • A. Conduct a baseline cardiovascular risk assessment before initiating cancer treatment.
  • B. Schedule cardiac function screening at appropriate intervals during and after treatment.
  • C. Prevention of cardiotoxicity:
    • Track cumulative anthracycline doses and discontinue treatment when maximum dose is reached:
      • Doxorubicin: 550 mg/m²
      • Daunorubicin (Cerubidine, DaunoXome): 600 mg/m²
      • Mitoxantrone (Novantrone): 160 mg/m²
      • High-dose cyclophosphamide: 144 mg/kg over 4 days
  • D. Monitor baseline and follow-up ECG or MUGA scans to assess cardiac function.
  • E. Monitor vital signs and body weight regularly.
  • F. Track radiation field and dose to minimize cardiac toxicity, especially in thoracic cancers.
  • G. Patient education:
    • Teach patients which signs and symptoms (e.g., dyspnea, edema, palpitations) to report immediately.
    • Promote a heart-healthy diet, adjusting for any treatment-related dietary restrictions.
    • Encourage participation in a medically supervised exercise program to maintain cardiovascular health and overall well-being.
Nursing Interventions
ConditionNursing Interventions
Cardiovascular Toxicity (from cancer therapy)
  • Baseline & ongoing monitoring: ECG, ECHO (LVEF), BP, cardiac biomarkers (troponin, BNP).
  • Monitor for dyspnea, chest pain, palpitations, edema.
  • Educate patient on reporting new symptoms (fatigue, SOB, swelling).
  • Administer cardioprotective drugs as prescribed (e.g., dexrazoxane with anthracyclines).
  • Manage comorbidities (HTN, diabetes, CAD).
  • Support adherence to dose limits and schedule follow-up with cardio-oncology.
  • Energy conservation and psychological support.
Malignant Pericardial Effusion
  • Monitor vital signs & hemodynamic status (tachycardia, hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds).
  • Position: Elevate HOB to relieve dyspnea.
  • Administer oxygen as indicated.
  • Prepare & assist with pericardiocentesis / pericardial drain.
  • Pain management & anxiety reduction.
  • Educate patient about symptoms of tamponade (SOB, chest pain, lightheadedness).
  • Encourage activity modification to conserve energy.
  • Collaborate with palliative care if advanced disease.
Generalized Edema (e.g., due to HF, hypoalbuminemia, or therapy side effects)
  • Monitor daily weight, I&O, and limb girth.
  • Elevate extremities to reduce swelling.
  • Restrict sodium & fluids as prescribed.
  • Administer diuretics and monitor electrolytes.
  • Skin care: prevent breakdown from fluid overload.
  • Encourage mobility & gentle exercises.
  • Emotional support (edema may affect body image).
Lymphedema (secondary to cancer therapy / surgery)
  • Early identification and referral to lymphedema specialist.
  • Decongestive therapy: manual lymph drainage, compression bandaging, skin care, exercise.
  • Teach patient about avoiding trauma/injury to affected limb (no BP cuff, no injections).
  • Elevate limb when resting.
  • Educate about weight management & exercise.
  • Support hose / compression garments as prescribed.
  • Psychological support (coping with chronic condition).
