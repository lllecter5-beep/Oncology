Global Statistics

  • Most common cancer in women globally — 2.3 million new cases in 2020 (WHO)
  • Second leading cause of cancer deaths in women in developed countries (after lung cancer); number one in many developing countries
  • 30% of all new cancer cases in women worldwide
  • Death rate has declined significantly since 1989 due to improved screening and systemic treatments — attributed to mammography + advances in chemotherapy and targeted therapy
  • Between 2002–2003: 7% drop in incidence due to reduction in HRT use following Women's Health Initiative results
  • Breast cancer survivors are the largest group of cancer survivors
  • 79% of breast cancers occur in women ≥50 years of age (CNPP)

Saudi Arabia Statistics 🇸🇦

  • Most common cancer in Saudi women — accounts for ~28% of all female cancers (Saudi Cancer Registry)
  • Average age at diagnosis: 48–50 years — approximately 10 years YOUNGER than Western women
  • Higher proportion diagnosed at advanced stages (Stage III–IV) compared to Western countries — due to delayed presentation and limited screening uptake
  • Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) appears to have higher prevalence in Saudi women than in Western populations
  • 5-year survival improving with increased awareness and National Cancer Screening Program (NCSP)
  • Saudi Vision 2030 Health Sector Transformation Program includes breast cancer early detection as a key target

Male Breast Cancer

  • 100× less common than female BC — accounts for <1% of all breast cancers
  • Average age at diagnosis: 65 years (older than female average)
  • 5-year survival (all stages): 84% — Stage 0&I: 100% · Stage II: 91% · Stage III: 72% · Stage IV: 20%
  • Often diagnosed at later stage due to lack of awareness and screening
  • 4–40% of male BC have an identifiable genetic abnormality
  • 1 in 5 men with BC has a positive family history
  • BRCA1: 1/100 lifetime risk; BRCA2: 6/100 lifetime risk (60× general population)
  • Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) → ↑ estrogen → ↑ BC risk (20–50× general male population)
  • All men diagnosed with BC must undergo multigene genetic testing (NCCN)

🫀 Anatomy & Physiology

Breast Structure

  • Overlies chest (pectoral) muscles; lies between sternum and midaxillary line, 2nd–6th ribs, below clavicle
  • Connective tissue (collagen + elastin)
  • Nerves: T4 thoracic spinal nerve → innervates nipple and areola
  • Blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, fat, lobules, ducts
  • Suspensory Cooper's ligaments
  • Nipple and areola complex

Functional vs. Anatomic Breast

  • Glandular tissue: 14–18 lactiferous lobes → TDLUs (2.0–4.55mm ducts) → produce fatty breast milk
  • Adipose ratio: 2:1 (glands:fat) in lactating; 1:1 in non-lactating; fat ↑ post-menopause
  • Size and shape vary woman to woman; change throughout life
  • Men may develop breast tissue = gynecomastia

Lymphatic Drainage — Clinically Critical

  • 75% of lymph fluid → axilla (pectoral, subscapular, humeral nodes — Levels I, II, III)
  • 25% → parasternal nodes, contralateral breast, or abdomen
  • Also: infraclavicular and supraclavicular nodes
  • Internal mammary nodes involved in ~25% of upper inner quadrant tumors; 15% of outer quadrant
  • Axillary node status = single most important prognostic indicator
  • ⚠️ Exam Favorite: السؤال المتكرر — "أهم عامل تنبؤي (prognostic) في سرطان الثدي؟" الجواب: Axillary lymph node status.

⚡ Absolute vs. Relative Risk — Exam Distinction

  • Absolute risk: The actual probability of developing cancer over a specific time period in the general population. Example: 12% lifetime risk of breast cancer for average-risk women.
  • Relative risk (RR): Compares the incidence in a group with a risk factor to a group without it. Example: BRCA1 carriers have an RR of ~7–10× compared to general population.
  • Attributable risk: The amount of disease in the population that could be PREVENTED by removing a specific risk factor. Most useful for public health planning.
  • Nurses must be able to explain these concepts clearly — patients often confuse relative risk (sounds large) with absolute risk (actual change in probability).

🧬 Genetics & Hereditary Syndromes ⭐⭐⭐

BRCA1 & BRCA2 Basics

  • BRCA1: chromosome 17q21
  • BRCA2: chromosome 13q12.3
  • Both: tumor suppressor genes — mutation → DNA damage not repaired → cancer
  • Autosomal dominant; more common in Ashkenazi Jewish women
  • BRCA1 lifetime breast risk: 55–85%
  • BRCA2 lifetime breast risk: 45–85%
  • BRCA1 ovarian risk: 39–46%; BRCA2: 11–17%
  • BRCA1 → often TNBC (basal-like); BRCA2 → often ER+ adenocarcinoma
  • Bilateral prophylactic oophorectomy → ~80% ↓ ovarian/fallopian/peritoneal cancer

Male BC Genetics

  • 4–40% of male BC have a genetic abnormality
  • 1 in 5 men with BC has a family history
  • BRCA1: 1/100 risk; BRCA2: 6/100 risk
  • Klinefelter syndrome (extra X chromosome) → ↑ estrogen → ↑ BC risk
  • All men diagnosed with BC must undergo multigene genetic testing
Indications for Genetic Testing Referral
  • BC before age 50 · TNBC any age · Ovarian cancer any age
  • Male BC · ≥2 primary BCs · BC + ovarian cancer in same person
  • Pancreatic or high-grade prostate cancer
  • Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry
  • FDR with BC before age 45

Risk Management for BRCA Carriers

Surveillance
  • Monthly self-breast awareness from age 18
  • Clinical exam every 6 months from age 30
  • Annual mammography from age 30
  • Annual MRI days 7–15 of cycle (premenopausal)
  • MRI sensitivity: 94% vs. mammography 9% in BRCA carriers
Risk-Reducing Surgery
  • RRBM (Risk-Reducing Bilateral Mastectomy) → ↓ breast risk ~80–90%
  • RRSO (Risk-Reducing Salpingo-Oophorectomy) → ↓ ovarian risk 80–85%; also ↓ breast risk ~50%
  • RRSO recommended: age 35–40 or after childbearing
  • Residual risk: 1–4% Primary Peritoneal Carcinoma (PPC)
  • Tamoxifen: 44–50% ↓ contralateral BC in BRCA carriers

🛡️ Prevention

Primary · Chemoprevention · Surgical

Primary Prevention — Lifestyle Modifications

  • Healthy weight: obesity after menopause → ↑ estrogen in fat tissue → ↑ risk. Weight loss + healthy BMI ↓ risk
  • Physical activity: ≥150 min/week moderate-vigorous exercise → ↓ hormone levels + ↑ immune function → ↓ risk 20–80%
  • Alcohol: <1 drink/day. Strongest modifiable risk factor. 3–4 drinks/day = 30% ↑ risk
  • Smoking cessation: especially important in premenopausal women
  • Diet: ↑ fruits, vegetables, fiber, low-fat dairy. Limit red meat, processed foods, high sugar. Monounsaturated fats (olive oil) may be protective
  • Stress reduction: mindfulness and psychological support (indirect link to risk)
Protective Factors: Breastfeeding (long duration) · First pregnancy before age 20 · Higher number of births · Regular physical exercise · Healthy body weight throughout life

Bilateral Prophylactic Mastectomy (Risk-Reducing Bilateral Mastectomy (RRBM))

  • ↓ Breast Cancer (BC) risk >90% — indicated for BRCA mutations or very strong family history
  • Removes most breast tissue
  • Psychological impact, loss of sensation, body image changes must be addressed
  • Studies show women chose it for risk reduction and peace of mind — not necessarily for improved survival

Bilateral Salpingo-Oophorectomy (Risk-Reducing Salpingo-Oophorectomy (RRSO))

  • Recommended for women with BRCA mutations
  • ↓ ovarian cancer risk ~80–85% · ↓ breast cancer risk ~50%
  • Recommended age: 35–40 or after childbearing completed
  • Causes immediate menopause → cardiovascular and osteoporosis risk must be managed carefully
  • Residual risk: 1–4% Primary Peritoneal Carcinoma (Primary Peritoneal Carcinoma (PPC))

Mammography

  • Mammography is the standard primary screening tool for breast cancer because it has been shown in multiple randomized controlled trials to reduce breast cancer mortality by 20–30% in average-risk women when used for regular screening.
  • 3D mammography (digital breast tomosynthesis) creates a three-dimensional reconstruction of the breast and has been shown to reduce false-positive rates and recall (callback) rates compared to standard 2D mammography, particularly in women with dense breast tissue.
  • Despite widespread adoption, digital mammography has NOT been shown to be significantly superior to traditional (film-screen) mammography in reducing breast cancer mortality in randomized controlled trials, though it may perform better in younger women with dense breasts.

Ultrasonography

  • Ultrasound is primarily used as a supplemental screening tool in women with dense breast tissue, where mammography sensitivity is reduced. It can detect additional cancers in dense breasts but has a higher false-positive rate, leading to more unnecessary biopsies.
  • Differentiates solid vs. cystic
  • Does NOT replace mammography

MRI Breast

  • Sensitivity: ~94%
  • BRCA+ surveillance; staging; response assessment
  • Best days 7–15 of cycle (premenopausal)
Breast Self-Awareness (BSA): BSE as a formal monthly exam is no longer officially recommended by major guidelines due to lack of evidence in mortality reduction and high false-positive rates. However, women should be familiar with how their breasts normally feel and report any changes promptly. Clinical Breast Examination (CBE): every 3 years (age 20–39), then annually from age 40. High-risk women: twice-yearly Clinical Breast Examination (CBE). Symptoms requiring immediate evaluation: new lump, nipple discharge (especially bloody), skin changes (peau d'orange), nipple inversion, unexplained pain.

📋 BI-RADS Classification — Complete Guide (Exam Favorite)

  • BI-RADS 0: Incomplete — additional imaging needed (comparison with prior or supplemental views)
  • BI-RADS 1: Negative — routine annual screening
  • BI-RADS 2: Benign finding — routine annual screening (fibroadenoma, cyst, calcified lymph node)
  • BI-RADS 3: Probably benign — <2% malignancy risk → 6-month short-interval follow-up (not biopsy)
  • BI-RADS 4: Suspicious — biopsy recommended (4A: low suspicion 2–10%, 4B: moderate 10–50%, 4C: high 50–95%)
  • BI-RADS 5: Highly suggestive of malignancy — ≥95% probability → biopsy required
  • BI-RADS 6: Known biopsy-proven malignancy — used for follow-up imaging during treatment

⚠️ Key rule: BI-RADS 3 = follow-up NOT biopsy. BI-RADS 4 and 5 = biopsy needed. BI-RADS 6 = cancer already confirmed.

🔬 Breast MRI — Indications & Key Facts

  • MRI sensitivity in Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2)/BRCA1 (Breast Cancer gene 1) carriers: 94% vs mammography sensitivity of only 9% in dense BRCA1 (Breast Cancer gene 1) carriers — this is why MRI is essential for high-risk women
  • Indications for annual MRI: BRCA1/2 mutation carriers; first-degree relatives of untested BRCA carriers; lifetime risk >20–25%; prior chest radiation between ages 10–30; Li-Fraumeni, Cowden, or Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndromes
  • MRI is NOT recommended for average-risk women as a routine screening tool — too many false positives leading to unnecessary biopsies
  • Contrast-enhanced MRI: best performed on days 7–15 of menstrual cycle (premenopausal women) to reduce background parenchymal enhancement
  • MRI can detect contralateral breast cancer in newly diagnosed patients (~3–5% of cases) — changes surgical planning
📌 من الكتاب الأزرق — Accelerated Partial Breast Irradiation (APBI):
  • The MammoSite system delivers high-dose radiation directly to the site of tumor excision and targets the area where cancer is most likely to recur. This is a form of Accelerated Partial Breast Irradiation (APBI) — an alternative to whole breast radiotherapy after lumpectomy in selected low-risk patients. Treatment is shorter (typically 1 week vs. 6 weeks for standard radiotherapy). CNPP, Pages ~1308-1310.
  • Breast cancer recurrence is possible 15 years or even longer after the initial diagnosis — patients should be followed indefinitely by an oncologist or PCP experienced in breast cancer survivor surveillance. CNPP, Page ~1337.

🔴 Pathogenesis

Mutation Accumulation · Tumor Heterogeneity · ADH · ALH

How Breast Cancer Develops

  • Starts with a single cell that accumulates multiple genetic mutations → learns to proliferate rapidly without normal controls
  • Each tumor = heterogeneous cell population — each cell has different proliferation rate, metastatic potential, and treatment sensitivity
  • "Fittest" cells survive therapy → drives treatment resistance and recurrence
  • Benign lesions: Nonproliferative (simple cysts) = NOT a Breast Cancer (BC) risk factor. Proliferative lesions with atypia = significantly ↑ risk of future invasive Breast Cancer (BC)

🧬 Oncotype DX Recurrence Score — High-Yield Exam Topic

  • What it is: A 21-gene reverse transcriptase-PCR (RT-PCR) assay performed on tumor tissue (FFPE) that predicts the risk of distant recurrence and chemotherapy benefit in early-stage ER+/Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2)-negative breast cancer
  • Score interpretation (TAILORx trial — 2018):
    • Score 0–25: Low-intermediate risk → endocrine therapy alone is sufficient (no chemotherapy benefit). This applies to postmenopausal women; premenopausal women age ≤50 with score 16–25 may benefit from chemotherapy.
    • Score 26–100: High risk → chemotherapy + endocrine therapy recommended
  • Important: Oncotype DX does NOT assess distant recurrence risk based on genetic (hereditary) factors — it reflects the biology of the individual tumor
  • Other genomic tests: MammaPrint (70-gene assay, MINDACT trial), Prosigna/PAM50 (50-gene)

🔬 HER2 Testing Methods — How Pathologists Determine HER2 Status

  • IHC (Immunohistochemistry): Tests protein expression. Results: 0 or 1+ = HER2 negative; 2+ = equivocal (needs reflex FISH); 3+ = HER2 positive
  • FISH (Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization): Tests gene amplification. HER2:CEP17 ratio ≥2.0 OR average HER2 copy number ≥6 signals = positive. Used to confirm equivocal IHC 2+ results
  • HER2-low: New emerging category — IHC 1+ or IHC 2+/FISH-negative. Treated with trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd/Enhertu) — a newer ADC showing benefit in HER2-low metastatic disease
  • All invasive breast cancers must have ER, PR, and Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2) testing done — ASCO/CAP guidelines
📌 Hormone Receptor Status — Clinical Decision Guide: ER/PR and Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2) status determine which systemic therapies are appropriate. ER+ tumors (approx. 70% of all Breast Cancer (BC)) respond to endocrine therapy (tamoxifen or Aromatase Inhibitors (AIs)). Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2)+ tumors (approx. 15–20%) respond to targeted anti-Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2) therapy (trastuzumab, pertuzumab, T-DM1, lapatinib). Triple-negative Breast Cancer (BC) (15–20%) lacks all three receptors — chemotherapy remains the backbone, with emerging options including PARP inhibitors (BRCA-mutated), immunotherapy (pembrolizumab), and ADCs (sacituzumab govitecan). Biomarker testing (ER, PR, Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2), Ki-67 (proliferation index)) must be performed on ALL newly diagnosed breast cancers.

  • Change in breast size, shape, or contour

📌 من الكتاب الأزرق — معلومات إضافية عن الـ Diagnosis والـ Biomarkers:
  • EGFR and ER inverse correlation: There is an inverse correlation between Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) expression and Estrogen Receptor (ER) status — ER-negative tumors tend to have a higher level of EGFR than ER-positive tumors. Therapy that interferes with EGFR signaling provides therapeutic benefit in women who overexpress EGFR (ER-negative tumors). This explains why EGFR-targeted therapies are more relevant in Triple-Negative Breast Cancer (TNBC). CNPP, Page 1306.
  • Chest radiation risk: The risk of breast cancer correlates with increased radiation dosage, especially if a woman is exposed to radiation during young adulthood (puberty to age 30). Women treated for Hodgkin lymphoma with chest radiation are at particularly high risk and should begin annual MRI + mammography screening starting 8–10 years after radiation or at age 25, whichever comes later. CNPP, Page 371.
📌 Breast Cancer in Special Populations — Imaging Choices (CNPP Pages 1301–1303):
  • Pregnant woman with breast mass: Ultrasound is the imaging modality of choice — requires NO ionizing radiation. Fine-needle aspiration is also appropriate. Mammography may be used with abdominal shielding if ultrasound is inconclusive. MRI without gadolinium contrast may also be used. Biopsy (core needle) is safe at any trimester under local anesthesia.
  • Young woman (<30) with breast mass: Ultrasound is preferred over mammography as first-line imaging due to dense breast tissue and avoidance of unnecessary radiation exposure in young women.
  • Lactating woman with breast mass: Ultrasound is the preferred initial imaging modality. Mammography can be performed but milk-filled ducts may obscure findings. Recommend pumping before mammogram to improve image quality.
  • Pregnancy-Associated Breast Cancer (PABC): Defined as breast cancer diagnosed during pregnancy or within 1 year postpartum. Often diagnosed at a later stage due to masking by normal breast changes. Prognosis is similar to non-pregnant women when matched by stage. Chemotherapy can be given safely after the first trimester (organogenesis complete). Radiation, hormonal therapy, and trastuzumab are contraindicated during pregnancy. Surgery is safe at any trimester.

📌 8th Edition AJCC Staging — Key Changes (Exam-Tested)

  • Anatomic vs. Clinical Prognostic Stage: The 8th edition introduced clinical prognostic stage groups that incorporate biomarkers (ER, PR, Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2), grade, Oncotype DX) in addition to Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM). 📌 Cardiac Monitoring Requirements for Breast Cancer Treatments:
  • Anthracyclines (doxorubicin/epirubicin): Dose-dependent cardiotoxicity. Maximum cumulative doxorubicin dose: 550 mg/m² (450 mg/m² if prior chest radiation). Baseline Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF) required before starting. Monitor for decreased Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF), Congestive Heart Failure (CHF), arrhythmias. Risk increases with cumulative dose.
  • Trastuzumab (Herceptin): Reversible cardiotoxicity (unlike anthracyclines). Check Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF) before starting, every 3 months during treatment. Hold if Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF) drops >10% from baseline OR falls below 50%. Unlike anthracycline cardiotoxicity, trastuzumab cardiotoxicity is usually reversible upon discontinuation.
  • Pertuzumab: Also requires baseline Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF) assessment. Similar cardiac monitoring as trastuzumab.

  • LHRH Agonists (Goserelin/Zoladex®, Leuprolide/Lupron Depot®):

💊 PARP Inhibitors — ONS Nursing Considerations

  • Olaparib (Lynparza®) PO: 300 mg TWICE daily continuously. With or without food. Tablets — do NOT chew, crush, dissolve, or split. If miss dose → skip it. 💊 Other Chemotherapy Agents for Breast Cancer — ONS Notes

  • Eribulin (Halaven®) IV: Metastatic breast cancer (after ≥2 prior chemotherapy regimens). Dose-limiting toxicities: neutropenia, peripheral neuropathy. 💊 Pembrolizumab (Keytruda®) — ONS Nursing Considerations

  • Dosing: 200 mg IV every 3 weeks OR 400 mg IV every 6 weeks (flat dosing)
  • Breast cancer indications:
    • High-risk early-stage TNBC — neoadjuvant pembrolizumab + chemo → surgery → adjuvant pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-522) — regardless of PD-L1 status
    • PD-L1+ (CPS ≥10) metastatic TNBC — first-line + chemotherapy (KEYNOTE-355)
  • Immune-related adverse events (irAEs) — nurse must recognize early:
    • Pneumonitis: new cough, dyspnea, chest pain → HOLD drug, systemic steroids
    • Colitis: diarrhea, abdominal pain, blood in stool → HOLD drug, steroids
    • Hepatitis: elevated LFTs, jaundice → HOLD drug, steroids
    • Endocrinopathies (most common): hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, hypophysitis, type 1 diabetes → hormone replacement, may be PERMANENT
    • Dermatitis: rash, pruritus (common, usually mild)
    • Nephritis: elevated creatinine → HOLD drug, steroids
  • irAE management: Grade 1 = continue + monitor. Grade 2 = hold + consider steroids. Grade 3–4 = permanently DISCONTINUE + high-dose systemic steroids
  • Key nursing teaching: Report ANY new symptom — irAEs can occur during OR months AFTER treatment completion. Carry steroid card. Inform ALL healthcare providers about immunotherapy.
  • ⚠️ Unlike chemotherapy, irAEs can occur at any time — even months after treatment completion

G — Bone-Modifying Agents

Zoledronic Acid (Bisphosphonate)
  • Inhibits osteoclast activity → ↓ bone resorption → ↓ bone pain, fractures, hypercalcemia

  • RANK-ligand (RANKL) inhibitor — prevents osteoclast maturation
  • Subcutaneous injection; used for bone metastases prevention and treatment
  • SE: Hypocalcemia; ONJ; atypical femur fractures

  • Monitor serum calcium during therapy

📌 Common Sites of Breast Cancer Metastasis (in order of frequency): (1) Bone — most common (~70%); presents with pain, hypercalcemia, pathologic fractures, and spinal cord compression. Bone metastases from breast cancer are typically osteolytic (bone-destroying). (2) Lung — presents with dyspnea, cough, pleural effusion. (3) Liver — presents with right upper quadrant pain, jaundice, elevated Liver Function Tests (LFTs), hepatomegaly. (4) Brain — presents with headache, seizures, neurologic deficits; more common in Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2)+ and Triple-Negative Breast Cancer (TNBC). Brain metastases are treated with Whole-Brain Radiation Therapy (WBRT) (≥4 lesions, palliative) or stereotactic radiosurgery (1–4 lesions, more targeted). Spinal cord compression is a medical emergency — sudden back pain + leg weakness = STAT imaging + dexamethasone + oncology consultation.
📌 Hypercalcemia of Malignancy — Nursing Priority: Most common metabolic emergency in cancer. Caused by osteolytic bone metastases releasing calcium. Symptoms (remember "Bones, Stones, Moans, Groans, Psychic Overtones"): bone pain, kidney stones/polyuria, nausea/constipation, muscle weakness/fatigue, confusion/depression. Treatment: IV normal saline hydration (primary) → bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid) or denosumab → calcitonin for rapid reduction. Nursing: monitor calcium levels, urine output, neurological status, ECG changes (shortened QT interval).
Re-biopsy at recurrence is recommended: Receptor status may change from primary tumor to metastasis. Always re-confirm ER, PR, Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2), Ki-67 (proliferation index) — this may change the treatment strategy entirely.

Pre-Operative & Post-Operative Care

Pre-Op
  • Assess anxiety level — pre-surgical anxiety ↑ biophysical stress → may impede healing
  • Educate about procedure, expected outcomes, reconstruction options
  • Discuss body image concerns before surgery

📌 Lymphedema — Key Clinical Facts: Lymphedema occurs in 6–30% of patients after axillary lymph node dissection (Axillary Lymph Node Dissection (ALND)) and in 0–3% after sentinel lymph node biopsy (Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy (SLNB)). It is caused by disruption of lymphatic drainage, leading to protein-rich fluid accumulation. It is a chronic, progressive condition with no cure — management focuses on control. Complete Decongestive Therapy (Complete Decongestive Therapy (CDT)) is the gold standard: manual lymphatic drainage (Manual Lymphatic Drainage (MLD)) + multilayer compression bandaging + therapeutic exercise + skin care. Nurses must teach prevention: avoid blood pressure measurement, venipuncture, and constricting clothing on the affected arm. Report any sudden increase in swelling, redness, or warmth (signs of cellulitis — a serious lymphedema complication).
Post-Mastectomy
  • Wound care + maintain functioning wound drains
  • Blocked drains → seroma/hematoma → infection → flap necrosis
  • Exercises begin as early as 24 hours post-op
  • Affected arm in adducted position; limited wrist and elbow exercises
  • Flex fingers; touch hand to shoulder; NO ball squeezing — ↑ blood flow → ↑ swelling
  • Educate about altered sensations: numbness, tingling, phantom breast sensation — may persist indefinitely

  • No BP cuffs, blood draws, or injections in affected arm
  • Teach arm circumference measurement — notify provider if increases
  • Compression therapy and manual lymphatic drainage
  • Protect arm from injury and infection
  • Monitor for cellulitis
  • Refer to lymphedema therapist

Chemotherapy Nursing Care

  • Monitor CBC and vital signs before each cycle
  • Manage nausea/vomiting with antiemetics
  • Encourage adequate hydration and nutrition
  • Educate about infection prevention (hand hygiene, avoid crowds)

  • Monitor for peripheral neuropathy (taxanes) and cardiotoxicity (anthracyclines)

📌 Chemotherapy-Induced Peripheral Neuropathy (Chemotherapy-Induced Peripheral Neuropathy (CIPN)): Commonly caused by taxanes (paclitaxel, docetaxel) and platinum agents. Presents as numbness, tingling, burning pain, or weakness in hands and feet (stocking-glove distribution). Chemotherapy-Induced Peripheral Neuropathy (CIPN) can be dose-limiting, requiring dose reductions or discontinuation. Assessment: use validated tools such as the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grading scale. Management: duloxetine (Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors (SNRIs)) have the strongest evidence for treatment. Prevention: dose modifications when symptoms develop. Teach patients: report early symptoms before they progress; avoid falls due to sensory loss; protect feet (no bare feet).

🩺 Oncology Emergencies in Breast Cancer — Nursing Response

  • Spinal Cord Compression (SCC): Back pain + leg weakness/numbness + bladder/bowel dysfunction in a patient with bone mets = EMERGENCY. Action: STAT MRI spine, IV dexamethasone (reduces edema), notify oncology/neurosurgery/radiation. Do NOT ambulate until cleared. Prognosis depends on speed of treatment — neurological deficits can be permanent if untreated.
  • Superior Vena Cava (SVC) Syndrome: Facial swelling, neck vein distension, dyspnea, headache worse when bending forward. Caused by mediastinal/supraclavicular node involvement. Treatment: radiation ± stenting ± anticoagulation. Raise HOB, avoid IVs in upper extremities.
  • Hypercalcemia of Malignancy: Ca >10.5 mg/dL (corrected). Most common metabolic emergency. Signs: confusion, polyuria, constipation, nausea, ECG changes (short QT). Treatment: aggressive IV hydration with normal saline → bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid) → calcitonin for rapid effect.
  • Febrile Neutropenia: T ≥38.3°C + Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC) <500 = emergency. Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics within 60 minutes. Blood cultures × 2 before antibiotics. Do not delay for test results.

💬 Psychosocial Support — Key Nursing Interventions

  • Body image disturbance after mastectomy: Most commonly reported psychosocial issue. Nursing: therapeutic communication, peer support groups (e.g., Reach to Recovery), breast reconstruction options counseling, normalized discussion of sexuality and intimacy.
  • Fear of recurrence (FCR): One of the most persistent survivorship concerns. Evidence-based interventions: mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), validated FCR assessment tools (FCR-7, CARS).
  • Chemotherapy-induced cognitive impairment ('chemo brain'): Affects 17–75% of patients during/after treatment. Symptoms: difficulty with concentration, memory, processing speed. Management: cognitive rehabilitation, structured scheduling, adequate sleep, stress reduction.
  • Sexual dysfunction: Common with endocrine therapy (vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, decreased libido). Address proactively — nurses must initiate the conversation. Refer to sexual health specialist when needed.
  • Financial toxicity: Growing concern — cancer treatment costs can be catastrophic. Social work referral, patient assistance programs, and cost-of-care conversations are nursing responsibilities.

📋 Survivorship Care Plan (SCP) — Components

  • Treatment summary: Diagnoses, surgery type, chemotherapy regimens (cumulative doses), radiation fields, hormonal/targeted therapies
  • Surveillance schedule: Clinical breast exam every 3–6 months for 3 years, then every 6–12 months for 2 years, then annually. Annual mammography of remaining breast tissue. No routine tumor markers, CT scans, or bone scans for asymptomatic survivors (unless symptoms develop)
  • Late effects monitoring: Cardiac (anthracyclines/trastuzumab), bone density (AIs — DXA every 1–2 years), peripheral neuropathy (taxanes), lymphedema, cognitive function, mental health
  • Health promotion: Maintain healthy weight, exercise ≥150 min/week, limit alcohol (<1 drink/day), smoking cessation, calcium + vitamin D supplementation (especially on AIs)
  • Coordination: Shared care plan sent to primary care physician for ongoing management of long-term effects outside the oncology setting
  • Tertiary prevention / Lifestyle: Smoking cessation, maintain healthy weight (BMI <25), regular exercise (≥150 min/week), limit alcohol (<1 drink/day), stress management. These topics are critical but often under-discussed in daily oncology practice — nurse's role is essential.
  • Healthy People 2030 goals: Increase proportion of cancer survivors receiving care coordination and survivorship care plans from their oncologist team.
📌 من الكتاب الأزرق — Psychosocial & Survivorship:
  • Suicide risk: Research shows that of cancer patients who committed suicide, 36% did so within the first year of diagnosis, and 1 in 3 did so within one month of diagnosis. Suicide risk remains elevated even after successful treatment — oncology nurses must screen for distress and suicidal ideation at every visit. CNPP, Pages 2021–2022.
  • African American breast cancer survivors valued support groups that considered their unique cultural and socioeconomic positions, with emphasis on spirituality and religion resulting in better quality of life. Cultural competence is essential in psychosocial care. CNPP, Page 2021.
  • 50–75% of breast cancer survivors have one or more symptoms of urogenital dysfunction: dyspareunia, vaginal dryness, urinary tract infections, decreased libido, and loss of sexual satisfaction. This is the most underreported and undertreated survivorship issue. Proactive nursing assessment and referral to sexual health specialist are needed. CSM, Page 55.
  • Body image: Most empirical studies on cancer-related body image and quality of life have focused on women with breast cancer or gynecologic cancer — reflecting the significant impact of breast loss/alteration on self-concept and sexual identity. CNPP, Page 2019.
  • Radiation-related lymphedema prevention: Preventive measures after radiation to the breast, chest wall, and axilla include: avoid tight clothing, jewelry, and elastic bands on the affected arm; avoid extreme temperatures; avoid carrying heavy packages on the affected side; report any swelling, redness, or warmth promptly. CNPP, Pages 1312–1313.
